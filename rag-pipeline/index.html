Look It Up, Then Answer

How retrieval-augmented generation works, explained one step at a time, using the live research chat on this site as the worked example.

Reju Sam John Auckland, New Zealand September 2026 Try the live chat →

The home page of this site has a small chat box called Ask My Research. You type a question about my published papers and it answers in plain English, with a list of the papers it drew on. Behind it sits a technique called retrieval-augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG.

I teach on a Data Science & AI programme, and RAG is one of the ideas my learners ask about most. It is also one of the easiest to learn badly, because most tutorials jump straight to a library call. In this guide I take the opposite route. Each step is explained in plain words first, then given its proper name, then shown in the real code that runs the chat.

The corpus currently holds six of my peer-reviewed papers, split into 779 chunks. Those figures are as of September 2026; I am adding more publications, so they will grow.

1. Why a language model on its own isn't enough

A large language model learns from a huge snapshot of text, and then the snapshot stops. Anything written after that date, or never included in it, is simply not in the model. That date is called the training cut-off.

Suppose you ask a general chat model about one of my papers on Lassa virus in West African rodents. Three things can go wrong. The paper may be newer than the cut-off. Even if the model saw it, it cannot show you where its answer came from, because it has no citations to give. And when it doesn't know, it rarely says so. It produces fluent, confident text that fills the gap with whatever sounds plausible.

For research questions that last habit is the dangerous one. A wrong answer that sounds right is worse than no answer at all.

2. The big idea: look it up, then answer

Think of the difference between a closed-book and an open-book exam. In a closed-book exam you answer from memory. In an open-book exam you first find the relevant pages, then write your answer from them, and you can point to the page you used.

Retrieval-augmented generation turns a language model into an open-book student. In one sentence: before the model answers, the system searches your own documents for the passages most relevant to the question and gives those passages to the model, with an instruction to answer from them alone.

There are two phases. The first happens once, ahead of time: the papers are cut into pieces, each piece is turned into numbers, and the results are stored. The second happens every time someone asks a question:

3. Step 1: Splitting documents into chunks

A research paper is too long to search as a single block. If you boil a whole paper down to one summary, a question about one small detail has to compete with everything else the paper says. So the first job is to cut each paper into short passages. Each passage is called a chunk.

The pipeline makes chunks of at most 1000 characters, roughly a long paragraph. It tries to cut at natural boundaries: blank lines first, then line breaks, then sentence ends, then spaces. Only if none of those work does it cut mid-word. The tool that does this is LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter: 

# scripts/ingest_deploy.py
CHUNK_SIZE = 1000
CHUNK_OVERLAP = 200

splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(
    chunk_size=CHUNK_SIZE,
    chunk_overlap=CHUNK_OVERLAP,
    separators=["\n\n", "\n", ". ", " ", ""],
)
chunks = splitter.split_documents(docs)

The second setting, overlap, matters just as much. Each new chunk starts by repeating up to 200 characters from the end of the previous one. Why? Because an idea can straddle the cut. Without overlap, the first half of a sentence would sit in one chunk and the second half in the next, and neither would make sense on its own.

Here are two consecutive chunks from the vector store, taken from the start of my Lassa virus paper. I have quoted only the end of the first chunk and the start of the second. The highlighted text appears in both.

End of chunk A… Here, we developed a susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR)-based model of LASV dynamics in its rodent host, M. natalensis , with a persistently infected class and seasonal birthing to test the impact
Start of chunk B(SIR)-based model of LASV dynamics in its rodent host, M. natalensis , with a persistently infected class and seasonal birthing to test the impact of changes to seasonal birthing in the future owing to climate and land use change. …

Notice two things. First, the sentence that is cut at the end of chunk A is complete in chunk B, which is exactly what overlap is for. Second, the overlap here is 146 characters, not 200. The splitter only carries over whole pieces (in this case, whole lines), so 200 is a ceiling, not an exact amount. The stray space before the comma and the line breaks are left over from reading the PDF. Real chunks are messier than textbook examples.

4. Step 2: Turning text into numbers (embeddings)

Computers cannot compare meanings directly, but they can compare numbers. So each chunk is converted into a list of numbers chosen so that passages with similar meaning get similar lists. You can picture each list as the coordinates of a point: similar meaning ends up at nearby points. That list of numbers is called an embedding, and the model that produces it is an embedding model.

The chat uses all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a small model built for sentence similarity. It turns every chunk into 384 numbers, so each chunk becomes a point in a 384-dimensional space. It runs through a library called fastembed, which uses the ONNX runtime, so no PyTorch or GPU is needed on the server.

Nobody can picture 384 dimensions, but the maths is the same in two, so let's use two. How do we measure whether two arrows point the same way? We use cosine similarity: multiply matching coordinates and add them up (the dot product), then divide by the lengths of the two arrows. The answer is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are at right angles, and −1 when they point in opposite directions.

Working through it:

  • a and b. Dot product = 3×4 + 4×3 = 24. Both arrows have length 5, so cos(a, b) = 24 / 25 = 0.96. Very similar.
  • a and c. Dot product = 3×(−4) + 4×3 = 0, so cos(a, c) = 0 / 25 = 0. Unrelated.

In the real system the arrows have 384 coordinates instead of two, but the calculation is identical. A question about Lassa virus and a chunk about Lassa virus should end up like a and b.

Everything downstream assumes that similar text ends up close together in the vector space. If the embedding model breaks that promise for your subject, no amount of prompt writing will save you.

5. Step 3: The vector store

Once every chunk has an embedding, we need somewhere to keep them that can answer the question “which stored points are closest to this new point?” That is a vector store, and finding the closest points is called nearest-neighbour search.

The chat uses Chroma, which runs inside the API process and saves everything to a folder on disk. For every chunk it keeps three things:

  • the vector, the 384 numbers;
  • the text of the chunk, so it can be handed to the model;
  • the metadata, small labels such as which paper the chunk came from (source_file) and which page.

Today the store holds 779 chunks from 6 papers. That metadata turns out to be the most important part of the whole system for citations, as we will see in section 9.

One detail for the curious. Chroma in this setup ranks neighbours by straight-line (Euclidean) distance rather than by cosine similarity. That sounds like a contradiction, but the embeddings are normalised to length 1, and for vectors of length 1 the two measures always put neighbours in the same order.

6. Step 4: Retrieval

When a question arrives, it is embedded with the same model used for the chunks, and the vector store returns the closest chunks. How many it returns is a setting usually called k. In this pipeline it is 4: 

# rag_api/pipeline.py
RETRIEVER_K = 4

store.as_retriever(
    search_type="similarity", search_kwargs={"k": RETRIEVER_K}
)

Choosing k is a trade-off. If k is too small, the one passage that holds the answer may sit just outside the cut, and the model never sees it. If k is too large, the relevant passages are buried among loosely related ones, the prompt gets longer and slower, and the model has more chances to be distracted. Four chunks of up to 1000 characters gives the model a few paragraphs of reading, which suits short answers.

7. Step 5: The grounded prompt

Now we have a question and four passages. The next step is to write the instructions the model will follow. A prompt is grounded when it tells the model to answer only from supplied material. This is the actual template the chat uses. (In the code, some long lines are joined; the line breaks here are for reading.) 

# SYSTEM_TEMPLATE in rag_api/pipeline.py
You are a research assistant for Dr Reju Sam John, a computational
epidemiologist and data scientist based in Auckland, New Zealand.

INSTRUCTIONS:
- Answer the question using ONLY the provided context from his
published peer-reviewed papers.
- If the context does not contain enough information to answer,
say so honestly — do not hallucinate.
- Cite only the papers named in the [Source: ...] tags, using that
label in brackets, e.g. (John et al., 2024).
- Never cite references that appear inside the context text (for
example other authors the papers themselves cite).
- Write in plain prose without Markdown: no asterisks, bullet lists,
headings or bold text. Use short paragraphs separated by a blank
line if needed.
- Do not end with a list of sources or quotations; the sources are
shown to the reader separately.
- Be concise but thorough.
- Keep answers under 150 words for readability.

CONTEXT:
{context}

QUESTION:
{question}

ANSWER:

Line by line:

  • “You are a research assistant…” sets the role and tells the model whose work it is discussing.
  • “Answer … using ONLY the provided context” is the fence. It asks the model to ignore what it half-remembers from training.
  • “If the context does not contain enough information… say so honestly” is the escape route. Without it, a model that finds nothing useful tends to invent something rather than admit a gap.
  • “Cite only the papers named in the [Source: ...] tags” ties citations to labels the code controls. Each retrieved chunk is placed in the context under a tag such as [Source: Hayman et al., 2022]. Two of my papers are both “John et al., 2024”, so for those the code adds the first few words of the title to tell them apart.
  • “Never cite references that appear inside the context text” was added after a real problem, described in section 12.
  • The Markdown and source-list lines keep the answer as plain prose, because the chat box shows raw text and lists the sources itself.
  • “under 150 words” keeps answers readable in a small chat window.
  • {context} and {question} are placeholders. At run time the four tagged chunks and the user's question are pasted in.

8. Step 6: Generation

The filled-in prompt goes to the language model. It receives nothing else: no search engine, no memory of earlier questions, just the instructions, four passages and one question. The chat calls openai/gpt-oss-120b hosted by Groq, with openai/gpt-oss-20b as a fallback (more on that in section 11).

One setting deserves an explanation: temperature. A language model writes by repeatedly choosing the next word from a list of likely candidates. Temperature controls how adventurous that choice is. High values encourage variety, which suits creative writing. Low values make the model stick to its most likely choices. The chat uses 0.1: 

# rag_api/pipeline.py, inside build_pipeline
ChatGroq(
    model=model,
    temperature=0.1,
    timeout=settings.llm_timeout_s,
    max_retries=1,
)

For summarising research we want faithful and consistent rather than inventive, so a low temperature is the right choice. Here are two answers from the live chat:

From the live chat, September 2026

By altering the timing and magnitude of rodent births and the rates at which rodents and humans come into contact, these activities shift the basic reproductive number (R p) and can create asynchrony between peak rodent infection and peak human risk. The model was explicitly built to test how such anthropogenic changes modify virus persistence and transmission dynamics in the reservoir (John et al., 2024).

9. Citations you can trust

A citation is only useful if it is true. Language models are good at producing text that looks like a citation, which is not the same thing.

Here is a real example from this project. After I switched to the current model, some answers began citing works such as “Fichet‑Calvet et al., 2014” and “Safronetz et al., 2022”. Those are real papers, but they are not in my corpus. They appear inside my papers, in the reference lists and in-text citations that were retrieved along with everything else. A reader could easily have taken them for sources the system had actually used.

So the chat does not trust the model for the list of sources at all. The “Retrieved from” list under each answer is built in code from the metadata of the chunks that were actually retrieved. Each chunk carries its source_file label, and a small file called papers.json maps that label to the title, authors, year, journal and DOI. The function is short: 

# rag_api/sources.py
def resolve_sources(
    docs: Sequence[Document], papers: Mapping[str, Source]
) -> list[Source]:
    """Unique papers behind the retrieved chunks, in retrieval order."""
    seen: set[str] = set()
    sources: list[Source] = []
    for doc in docs:
        key = doc.metadata.get("source_file")
        if not key or key in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(key)
        source = papers.get(key)
        if source is None:
            logger.warning("No papers.json entry for source_file=%r", key)
            source = Source(title=key)
        sources.append(source)
    return sources

The model cannot add a paper to this list, because the list never passes through the model. A paper appears only if one of its chunks was retrieved.

The in-text citations in the answer itself are still written by the model, so they get a second check. After generation, a filter removes any bracketed author–year citation, such as “(Smith et al., 2019)”, that does not match one of the retrieved papers. Section 12 explains why an instruction in the prompt was not enough on its own, and what the filter does not yet catch.

10. How do we know it works?

“It gave a good answer when I tried it” is not evidence. You need a fixed set of questions, run the same way each time, with the results written down. I wrote 15 test questions: 12 that the papers should be able to answer, and 3 that they should not.

The first measure is the retrieval hit rate: for each answerable question, was the paper I expected among the retrieved chunks? The results, from eval/results.md in the repository:

  • Retrieval hit rate: 12/12 answerable questions, with k = 4.
  • Unanswerable questions: all 3 were declined. They asked about the adult dose of paracetamol, influenza vaccination rates in New Zealand schools, and the winner of the 2023 Rugby World Cup.
From the live chat, September 2026
Q: Who won the 2023 Rugby World Cup?
The provided context does not contain any information about the winner of the 2023 Rugby World Cup.
Retrieved from: Muylaert et al. (2023), Nature Communications; Hayman et al. (2022), Journal of The Royal Society Interface; Rulli et al. (2025), Reviews of Geophysics

Look closely at that last example. The retriever always returns its four nearest chunks, even when none of them is relevant. It has no notion of “nothing found”. The refusal comes from the prompt's escape route, not from the search.

What these numbers do not tell you

A perfect score deserves suspicion, and I want to be honest about this one.

  • 12/12 flatters the retriever. Most of my questions reuse words from the paper titles, which makes them easy to match. Real visitors phrase things differently.
  • A retrieval hit is not a good answer. In three cases (the questions labelled l1, b1 and s2) the right paper was retrieved, yet the answer said the retrieved passages did not contain the specific result asked for.
  • One run is a snapshot. Question l1 is the Lassa question shown in section 8. In the evaluation run the chat said the passages lacked the result; when the same question was asked again on the live site, it gave the answer shown there. Generation varies between runs, even at low temperature, which is another reason an evaluation needs repeated runs and a human review.
  • Fifteen questions is a small sample. It can catch a serious break, such as retrieval returning the wrong papers or the model inventing answers to unanswerable questions. It cannot give you a reliable accuracy figure.

A manual review of answer quality is still pending, because I am expanding the corpus with more publications and will re-run and review the evaluation after that. Until then, I am not quoting an accuracy percentage, and you should be wary of anyone who quotes one from a test this small.

11. Taking it to production

A notebook that works on your laptop is not a service. The moment strangers can reach it, a new set of questions appears. Here is what the chat does about each.

Cold starts

The API runs on Render's free tier, which shuts the server down after around 15 minutes without traffic. The next request has to wait while it starts again, roughly 30 to 60 seconds. Two things soften this. The API has a /health endpoint that only reports ready once the retrieval pipeline has loaded, and the home page sends a quiet wake-up request to it as soon as it opens, so the server is often warm by the time you have typed your question. If it is still starting, the chat box shows a “warming up” message instead of failing silently.

Secrets

Calling the model needs an API key. That key lives only in the hosting provider's environment settings. It is never in the website, where anyone could read it with the browser's developer tools, and never in the repository, where it would sit in the commit history for good. The browser talks to my API, and only my API talks to the model provider.

Limits

  • Question length: between 3 and 500 characters. Anything else is rejected before it reaches the model.
  • Rate limit: 10 requests per minute per client, so one visitor (or one script) cannot use up the model quota. Clients are told apart by the address Cloudflare reports for them. I checked that a request carrying a forged address header is rejected at Cloudflare's edge.
  • Timeout and fallback: each model call has 20 seconds. A call that times out or hits a temporary error is retried once. An error such as “model not found” is not retried: the request goes straight to the fallback model, openai/gpt-oss-20b, as it also does if the retry fails.
  • Error messages: when something does go wrong, the browser gets a short fixed message and a request ID, never internal detail. The ID lets me find the full error in the server logs.

Repeatable builds and checks

Every dependency is pinned to an exact version, so rebuilding the server cannot quietly pull in a new release that behaves differently. Each push to the repository runs continuous integration: a linter, the offline test suite, and a secret scan with gitleaks that fails the build if anything resembling a key has been committed. A separate set of live tests calls the real model and is run by hand.

Local and deployed

The project began as a local app and still has a local variant for offline experiments. The two use different models, which matters for the first failure in section 13.

12. Case study: the day the model disappeared (September 2026)

This month the chat stopped answering. Every question returned an HTTP 500 error, and the message behind it read: 

Error code: 404 - The model `llama-3.3-70b-versatile` does not exist or you do not have access to it. (model_not_found)

The provider had retired Llama 3.3 70B, the model the chat was built on. The name was written directly into the code, so there was nothing to fall back to. The embeddings, the vector store and the API key were all fine. One line was wrong, and the whole service was down.

A second problem hid inside the first. That message reached the browser because the old code passed raw error text straight through. This time it only revealed a model name, but internal error text can reveal things you would rather keep private.

The fix had four parts:

  • the model name moved out of the code and into configuration, so changing it no longer needs a code change;
  • the primary model became openai/gpt-oss-120b, with an automatic fallback to openai/gpt-oss-20b;
  • errors now return a short fixed message with a request ID;
  • a live smoke test calls both real models, so I can confirm they still exist without waiting for a visitor to find out.

The lesson: a hosted model is a dependency you do not control. Treat its name as configuration, plan for it to vanish, and test against the real thing.

A second incident: citations you didn't ask for

After the model switch, the citation problem from section 9 appeared. The new model cited works that appear inside my papers, such as “Fichet‑Calvet et al., 2014” and “Safronetz et al., 2022”. My first response was to add a line to the prompt telling it not to. On its own, that did not stop it.

The real fix was code. After the model answers, a deterministic filter reads every bracketed author–year citation and removes any that does not match a retrieved paper. The same step strips the Markdown symbols the model sometimes adds despite being asked for plain prose. The filter does not yet handle narrative citations written into the sentence, such as “Smith et al. (2019) showed…”; those are on the list for the next version.

The filter needed more care than I expected, because real names are untidy. Surnames can be hyphenated (and the model sometimes writes a non-standard hyphen character), carry accents, or start with lowercase particles such as “van der”. Each of those needed handling and a test.

The lesson: an instruction in the prompt shapes behaviour but does not guarantee it. If something must never happen, check for it in code, and be clear about exactly what that check covers.

13. Common ways RAG goes wrong

The embedding model has to match

The vector store is a collection of points made by one embedding model. If you build the store with one model and embed questions with another, the question lands in a completely different space from the chunks. The similarity scores become noise, and retrieval returns rubbish without any error message. That is why this project keeps two separate stores, chroma_db/ for the local nomic-embed-text variant and chroma_db_deploy/ for the deployed all-MiniLM-L6-v2 version. If you change the embedding model, re-embed everything.

Questions about the documents rather than in them

Some questions are about the documents themselves: “What methods did you use in the sarbecovirus study?” The embedding of that question is shaped by generic words such as methods and study, which appear in every paper. Retrieval can drift towards general methods text from several papers rather than the one section you meant.

The usual remedies are filtering by metadata (search only inside the named paper), rewriting the question with more specific terms before searching, or combining keyword search with embedding search, which is known as hybrid search. The chat does none of these yet.

The same weakness grows as a corpus spans more subjects. The word “network” means a contact network in epidemiology and something quite different in, say, physics. A single index gives it one blended meaning. Tagging each chunk with its field and searching within the right field keeps those meanings apart.

Prompt injection

Everything retrieved is pasted into the prompt, so text inside a document can try to act as an instruction: “ignore your previous instructions and…”. The same goes for the question itself. This is called prompt injection. A language model cannot reliably tell your instructions apart from instructions hidden in the material it is reading. The defences are to control what goes into the corpus (here, only my own published papers), to keep anything important, such as the source list, out of the model's hands, and to check the output in code, as the citation filter does.

14. Check your understanding

1. Why are the papers split into overlapping chunks instead of being embedded whole?

A single embedding of a whole paper blurs every topic in it together, so a question about one detail matches poorly. Short chunks each carry one idea. The overlap means a sentence cut at a chunk boundary still appears complete in the next chunk.

2. Two embeddings have a cosine similarity of 0. What does that tell you?

The vectors are at right angles, like a and c in section 4. For embeddings, that means the model sees the two pieces of text as unrelated in meaning.

3. You rebuild the vector store with a new embedding model but forget to change the model used for questions. What happens?

Questions and chunks now live in different vector spaces, so the distances between them mean nothing. Retrieval returns poorly matched chunks, with no error to warn you. Answers get worse or turn into refusals.

4. Why is the “Retrieved from” list built in code rather than taken from the model's answer?

Because the model can produce citations that look right but are wrong, including real papers that were never retrieved. Building the list from the retrieved chunks' metadata and papers.json guarantees that every listed paper was part of the context.

5. Retrieval found the right paper for 12 out of 12 questions. Does that mean the answers were correct?

No. In three cases the right paper was retrieved, yet the answer said the passages did not contain the specific result asked for. Retrieval hit rate measures whether the right paper was found, not whether the answer was right, and 15 questions is too few for an accuracy figure.

6. Why does the API key live on the server and not in the website's JavaScript?

Anything sent to a browser can be read by the person using it. A key in the page would let anyone use your model account. Keeping it in the server's environment settings, and out of the repository, means only the API can use it.

15. Try it yourself

The best way to understand RAG is to poke at one. Ask the chat something the papers cover, then something they don't, and compare the answers and the “Retrieved from” lists. Then read the code. It is small enough to follow in an afternoon.
