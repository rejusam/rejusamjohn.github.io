Memory

A model is given only the messages that fit in its context. See old messages drop out of the window, what that costs, and how a notepad keeps what matters.

About 10 minutes · Every trace and word count is computed in your browser; the rule is in The maths

Overview

In The loop and when to stop every model call was given every message so far. A real model cannot be given an endless list: there is a limit to how much text fits in at once.

The tiny model in Large language models, in miniature had a fixed window of characters, and real models count tokens (see Tokens). This page counts words instead, where a word is any piece of text between spaces. That is a simple stand-in: tokens are not the same as words.

This lesson gives the stand-in a budget. Each call is given the task and only the newest messages that fit. A fact found early can drop out before it is used, and then the run has to look it up again. A notepad, one more tool, keeps the facts where later calls can still use them.

You'll learn

  • what the context is, and how a budget sets which messages are in it
  • how a fact that drops out of the window can send a run in circles
  • what a notepad changes, and what it costs

  1. Start with no limit

    In the last lesson every model call was given every message so far. Start there, before any limit.

    Look at the trace. The task: ana and sam each buy 3 of the food they like. What do they pay in total?

    In each step the model is given the task and every message so far, then writes its next line. The run is done after 6 steps: "That costs $12.90."

    All the text a call is given is its context. A real model cannot be given endless text.

    Select a row. The picture shows, as word counts, what that step's call was given.

    Note: the model here is a stand-in that follows fixed rules (see What is an agent?). A real model can write wrong calls.

  2. Set a budget in words

    Now add a limit. Each call is given the task and only the newest messages that fit in a budget of words.

    Move the "budget (words)" slider by the "Run" button, or tick "no limit".

    The task is always given, and its 17 words are not counted. Then, from the newest message back, each message is added while the total fits the budget. The first that does not fit, and all older ones, are left out.

    The messages that fit are the window. In the trace, older rows outside it are greyed and tagged "no longer seen".

    At 54 words or more the run is done in 6 steps. From 8 to 52 it goes round until the step limit stops it.

    The maths

    Number the messages after the task m1,…,mtm_1, \dots, m_tm1​,…,mt​, newest last, and let BBB be the budget in words (on this page B is a budget, not a batch). The call is given the task and

    mj,…,mtwith j the smallest index such that∑i=jtwords(mi)≤Bm_j, \dots, m_t \quad\text{with } j \text{ the smallest index such that}\quad \sum_{i=j}^{t} \text{words}(m_i) \le Bmj​,…,mt​with j the smallest index such thati=j∑t​words(mi​)≤B

    Every message has at least one word, so the sum grows as jjj goes back. Taking the smallest such jjj is the same as stopping at the first message that does not fit. If even mtm_tmt​ does not fit, no jjj qualifies and the call is given only the task. With no limit, B=∞B = \inftyB=∞ and every message is kept.

    The slider runs from 6 to 60 words. At 6 words even ana's result (8 words) does not fit, so the second call is given only the task. It writes the same lookup again, and the repeat check stops the run after 2 steps.

  3. Going round in circles

    Now one run at 12 words. An early fact drops out of the window before it is used.

    Look at the trace. It opens on step 3's plan line (a short note of its next move). Greyed rows are not given to that step's call.

    Step 1 writes lookup("ana") and gets "ana likes apples and lives by the sea." Step 2 writes lookup("apples").

    Step 3's call is given 4 + 1 + 7 = 12 words, the whole budget. ana's result would make 12 + 8 = 20, so it is left out.

    Without ana's food, the stand-in writes lookup("ana") again (rule 3 of the six: look up a missing fact). Then it takes turns, ana, apples, ana, until the step limit stops it.

    The maths

    In the 10 steps the run writes lookup("ana") 5 times and lookup("apples") 5 times. sam is never looked up.

    The repeat check from The loop and when to stop does not catch this. It compares a call only with the one just before it, and here no two calls in a row are the same.

  4. A notepad keeps the facts

    Now switch the notepad on. The stand-in writes each fact down, and later calls are given the notes.

    Use the "notepad" switch by the "Run" button. The budget is still 12 words. The notes are listed under the bar.

    After each lookup, the next step writes the result in the notes (rule 2 of the six).

    The notes are given on top of the window. Step 9's call is given none of the lookup results, yet the facts are in the notes, so it writes the calculator call.

    The run is done after 10 steps, with 4 notes: "That costs $12.90."

    Note: here the notes do not count against the budget, a choice made for this page.

    The maths

    One step per note: 10 steps, 4 more than the 6 with no limit. That is why this lesson uses a step limit of 10; at the usual 8, the notepad run stops at the limit.

    In other designs the notes use up part of the budget.

    With the notepad on, every budget on the slider from 8 words up finishes. At 6 words ana's result drops out before the step that would write it down.

  5. Notes in real agents

    Last, the three runs side by side. A real model's context is limited too.

    Pick a run from the "run" menu. The picture shows all three runs of the pair task. The one in the trace has a thick outline.

    At 12 words the run with the notepad off never answers. With it on, it is done, in more steps than with no limit.

    A real language model can be given only so many tokens at once. So agents built around one often keep what matters as notes, or replace older messages with a short summary.

    Note: notes are not free. Here each one cost a step, and a note holds only what was written in it.

Quick check

At a budget of 12 words, why did the agent look ana up again?

Right. Step 3's call was given 4 + 1 + 7 = 12 words of newer messages; ana's result would have made 12 + 8 = 20.

lookup gives the same text every time: "ana likes apples and lives by the sea." ana was looked up again because the first result was no longer in the window.

The step limit only stops a run; it does not choose calls. ana was looked up again at step 3 of 10.

The repeat check only stops a run; it never writes a call. It did not fire here either, since no two calls in a row were the same.

What does the notepad change?

Right. At 12 words the notepad run is done after 10 steps with 4 notes.

The budget stays at 12 words. On this page the notes are kept outside the window, a choice made for this page.

It adds steps, one per note: 10 instead of 6 with no limit.

At 6 words ana's result drops out before it can be written down, and the repeat check stops the run after 2 steps.

Lesson complete

  • A model is given only its context: here the task and the newest messages that fit in a budget of words.
  • A fact that drops out of the window has to be found again; at 12 words the run went in circles until the step limit.
  • A notepad keeps facts for later calls, at the cost of a step per note.

Common misconceptions

  • "The model is given everything earlier in the run." Only what fits in its window: at 12 words, step 3's call was not given ana's result.
  • "Words and tokens are the same." Words here are a simple stand-in; real models count tokens.
  • "A notepad is free." Each note cost one step: 4 notes, 4 extra steps.
