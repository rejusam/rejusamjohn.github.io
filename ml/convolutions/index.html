Convolutions

A filter is a small grid of weights slid across an image. At each position it multiplies the pixels under it by its weights and adds them up, giving one number, large where the patch looks like the filter. The same nine weights are used everywhere.

About 10 minutes · Every map and sum is computed in your browser; the formulas are in The maths

Overview

The networks in hidden layers and going deeper connect every input to every unit. For an image that wastes weights: a vertical edge looks the same near the top corner as in the middle, so why learn to spot it separately in each place?

A convolution answers that with one small filter, here 3 × 3, slid over the whole image. This lesson does it step by step on a 12 × 12 image made up in your browser (see "About the data" at the end). Nothing is trained here; the next lesson lets a network learn its own filters.

You'll learn

  • how a filter turns each 3 × 3 patch of an image into one number of a feature map
  • how stride and padding set the size of the map, ⌊(n + 2p − f)/s⌋ + 1
  • why using the same weights at every position finds a pattern wherever it is, with far fewer weights

  1. An image is a grid of numbers

    To the computer this picture is 144 numbers in 12 rows and 12 columns: 0 is dark, 1 is bright and 0.5 is grey.

    There is a bright 4 × 4 square near the top left and a grey diagonal line lower down; every other pixel is 0. Move the cursor with the row and column sliders, or hover or tap on the image, and the panel on the right shows the numbers around it, as in data as numbers.

    Rows and columns are counted from 0, from the top left, here and in the rest of the lesson.

    The maths

    The image is a matrix XXX with entries X[r,c]X[r, c]X[r,c] for r,c=0,1,…,11r, c = 0, 1, \ldots, 11r,c=0,1,…,11. A colour image would have three such grids (red, green and blue); this one has a single grey channel.

  2. A filter, at one position

    The filter W is a 3 × 3 grid of weights. Lay it over a 3 × 3 patch, multiply each pixel by the weight on top of it, and add the nine products.

    This filter has −1 down its left column, 0 in the middle and +1 on the right, so the sum is the right column of the patch minus the left column. It is large when the patch is dark on the left and bright on the right: a vertical edge.

    The window starts at map row 2, column 0, which covers image rows 2–4 and columns 0–2. Its right column is inside the square (three 1s) and its left column is background (three 0s), so the sum is 3. Move the window with the sliders, or tap the image, and the products and the sum follow.

    The maths

    For the window at row rrr, column ccc (stride 1, no padding):

    Convolution here is cross-correlation (no flip): the filter is laid on the patch as written.   3. Slide it everywhere

    Do the same at every position, left to right and top to bottom. Each sum becomes one cell of the feature map.

    A 3 × 3 window fits in 10 positions across a row of 12 pixels, so the map is 10 × 10. Positive values are cyan; negative ones are violet and carry a minus sign. "Slide again" replays the sweep.

    The largest value, 3, is at map rows 2–3, columns 0–1: windows whose three rows sit in the square and that straddle its left edge. The smallest, −3, is at rows 2–3, columns 4–5, on the right edge, where bright turns to dark. Inside the square and on the plain background, left and right cancel and the map is 0. Windows that hold no pixel of the square see only the diagonal line, and give at most 0.5 in size.

With pixels between 0 and 1 it lies between −3 and 3, and it reaches 3 only when the right column is all 1 and the left column all 0.

  4. Other filters

    Pick a different filter, or type your own nine weights, and the map is recomputed.

    The horizontal-edge filter is the vertical one turned on its side. Its largest value, 3, is at rows 0–1, columns 2–3, on the square's top edge, and its smallest, −3, at rows 4–5, columns 2–3, on the bottom edge. Blur has every weight 1/9, so each value is the mean of the nine pixels: 1 where the window is inside the square, softer at its edges, and at most 0.17 along the thin line.

    "noisy" swaps in a speckled copy of the image. The vertical-edge filter still gives its largest value, 2.95, on the left edge, at row 3, column 0; where the clean image's map is 0, the noisy one stays within 0.51 of 0.

    "your own" opens nine boxes under the picture. Each takes a number from −2 to 2 in steps of 0.5; anything else is moved to the nearest allowed value when you leave the box. "Try the line filter" fills in 1 from top right to bottom left and −0.5 elsewhere. That filter looks like the line, and its map is largest along it: 1.5 there, against at most 1 on windows that touch the square.

    The maths

    Blur is W[i,j]=19\mathbf{W}[i, j] = \tfrac19W[i,j]=91​ for every i,ji, ji,j, so (X⋆W)[r,c]=19∑i,jX[r+i, c+j](X \star \mathbf{W})[r, c] = \tfrac19 \sum_{i,j} X[r + i,\, c + j](X⋆W)[r,c]=91​∑i,j​X[r+i,c+j], the mean of the patch. Each map value is a weighted sum, so the map is linear in the weights: doubling every weight doubles every value, and the map of the sum of two filters is the sum of their maps.

  5. Stride and padding

    The stride s is how far the window moves each time; padding p adds a ring of zeros around the image. Together they set the size of the map.

    With s = 1 and p = 0 the map is 10 × 10. Stride 2 skips every other position: 5 × 5. Padding 1 lets the window sit on the border, centred on each pixel in turn, so with stride 1 the map is 12 × 12, the same size as the image; with stride 2 it is 6 × 6. The dashed ring marks the zeros.

    The read-out works the size out from the formula, and the map drawn is counted from the actual sliding; for every setting here the two agree.

  6. The same weights everywhere

    The whole map came from just 9 weights, used again at every position. Shift the image and the map shifts with it.

    This is weight sharing. A layer that joined every one of the 144 pixels to every one of the 100 map values with its own weight would need 14,400 weights. The filter needs 9, because an edge is the same pattern wherever it is.

    Shift the image right by 2 and the largest value moves to rows 2–3, columns 2–3, two columns to the right, with the same value. This holds away from the edges: columns that come in from the left are new zeros, and anything pushed off the right edge is lost (with padding, the last column's window also changes). Here nothing is pushed off, so the whole map moves.

    A convolutional network stacks layers like this one and learns the weights of its filters instead of having them typed in. That is the next lesson.

Quick check

A 12 × 12 image, a 3 × 3 filter, stride 1, no padding: what size is the feature map?

Right. ⌊(12 + 0 − 3)/1⌋ + 1 = 10: the window's left column can sit on columns 0 to 9.

That needs padding 1, so the window can be centred on the border pixels. Without padding the window must fit inside the image, which leaves 10 positions.

12 − 3 = 9 counts the moves, not the positions: starting at column 0 and moving 9 times gives 10 positions.

That would be 12 ÷ 3, as if the windows did not overlap. With stride 1 they move one pixel at a time and overlap, so there are 10 positions per row.

Why use the same filter weights at every position?

Right. Here 9 shared weights did the work that a dense layer would need 14,400 weights for, and shifting the image shifted the map.

The map's size comes from n, f, s and p, not from sharing the weights.

Each map value sees only its 3 × 3 window. Sharing means the same weights are used for every window, not that a window sees more.

The weights are the same, but the patches differ, so the values differ: 3 on the left edge, 0 on flat patches.

Lesson complete

  • A filter is a small grid of weights; at each position the map value is the sum of the pixels times the weights, large where the patch looks like the filter.
  • The map has ⌊(n + 2p − f)/s⌋ + 1 rows and columns: 10 × 10 here with stride 1 and no padding.
  • The same 9 weights are used at every position, so a pattern is found wherever it is, and shifting the image shifts the map, away from the edges.

Common misconceptions

  • "The feature map is a smaller copy of the image." It is the filter's response: inside the bright square the vertical-edge map is 0, and it reaches 3 and −3 only on the square's sides.
  • "Convolution in a network flips the filter, as in a signal-processing textbook." Here it is cross-correlation: the filter is laid on the patch as written.
  • "Padding adds information about the border." It adds zeros, so the window can sit on the border pixels; with stride 1 and padding 1 the map stays 12 × 12.
