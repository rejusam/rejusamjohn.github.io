Support vector machines

Of all the straight lines that split two classes, pick the one with the widest empty street around it.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

Logistic regression and Naive Bayes both end with a boundary between two classes, and both get there through probabilities. A support vector machine (SVM) goes straight for the boundary, and chooses it by geometry: the line that keeps as far as it can from both classes.

One change of labels first. Earlier lessons wrote the classes as y = 0 and y = 1. In this lesson class 0 is y = −1 and class 1 is y = +1. The shapes stay the same: circles are class 0, now y = −1, and triangles are class 1, now y = +1. With these labels one test tells us whether a point is on its own side of the line, as The maths in step 3 shows.

All the points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • why an SVM picks the separating line with the widest street, and which points decide where it goes
  • how the setting C trades the street's width against points inside it when the classes overlap
  • how adding a new feature lets a straight-line method draw a curved boundary

  1. Many lines separate

    Many straight lines split these two classes. Which should we pick?

    Look at the plot: 24 points with two features, x₁ and x₂, 12 cyan circles (class 0) and 12 violet triangles (class 1).

    The solid line is the boundary between the classes. The dashed lines mark its street, the widest empty band around it: 1.98 wide.

    Turn the line with "tilt" and slide it with "shift". Some lines leave a narrow street. Some put points on the wrong side, and the read-out counts them.

    A support vector machine (SVM) picks the widest street, leaving room for new points that stray a little from their class.

    The maths

    A line with tilt φ\varphiφ has the unit normal n=(sin⁡φ, −cos⁡φ)\mathbf{n} = (\sin\varphi,\, -\cos\varphi)n=(sinφ,−cosφ), an arrow of length 1 at right angles to it. With shift ccc, the line is every point with

    n⋅x=c\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x} = cn⋅x=c

    and the distance from a point xi\mathbf{x}_ixi​ to the line is ∣n⋅xi−c∣\lvert \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i - c \rvert∣n⋅xi​−c∣. The street drawn has width 2min⁡i∣n⋅xi−c∣2\min_i \lvert \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i - c \rvert2mini​∣n⋅xi​−c∣, twice the distance to the nearest point.

    The sliders start at a tilt of 120° and a shift of 0.5.

  2. The margin

    Now measure the street.

    Look at the two dotted lines. They join the line to the nearest circle, 1.65 away, and the nearest triangle, 0.99 away. The read-out shows both.

    The street is the same width on both sides of the line, so it is twice the smaller distance, 1.98. This width is the margin.

    Move "shift" down, away from the triangle, until the two distances are about equal. The street is then the two distances added, 2.64: the read-out's "centred" row, the widest street at this tilt.

    Change "tilt" too: another tilt can give a wider street still.

    The maths

    Write d0d_0d0​ and d1d_1d1​ for the distances to the nearest circle and the nearest triangle. The street has width 2min⁡(d0,d1)2\min(d_0, d_1)2min(d0​,d1​). Sliding the line changes d0d_0d0​ and d1d_1d1​ by equal and opposite amounts while their sum stays the same, so the best place is the middle, with width d0+d1d_0 + d_1d0​+d1​.

    In this lesson the margin is the full width of the street. Some books, ISLR among them, use the word for half of it: the distance from the line to the nearest point.

  3. The widest street

    The SVM finds the widest street of all.

    Look at the new line, at a tilt of 144.4°. Its street is 3.61 wide, the widest of any straight line. This is the maximum-margin line.

    The ringed points on the street's edges, 2 circles and 1 triangle, are the support vectors. Each point is a vector of its two features, and these 3 hold the street in place.

    The SVM writes the line as w·x + b = 0, with w₁ = 0.32, w₂ = 0.45 and b = −0.03. Where w·x + b is above 0 it predicts a triangle (y = +1), and below 0 a circle (y = −1).

    The maths

    Multiplying w\mathbf{w}w and bbb by the same positive number does not move the line, so we choose the scale that makes yi(w⋅xi+b)=1y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1yi​(w⋅xi​+b)=1 for the nearest points. The distance from the line to a point is ∣w⋅x+b∣/∥w∥\lvert \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b \rvert / \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert∣w⋅x+b∣/∥w∥, so the street is

    margin=2∥w∥\text{margin} = \frac{2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert}margin=∥w∥2​

    wide. A wider street means a shorter w\mathbf{w}w, so the widest street solves

    min⁡w, b 12∥w∥2subject toyi(w⋅xi+b)≥1  for every i\min_{\mathbf{w},\, b}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \ \text{ for every } iw,bmin​ 21​∥w∥2subject toyi​(w⋅xi​+b)≥1  for every i

    This is the hard margin: no point may sit inside the street. It has an answer only when some line separates the classes.

    The street's edges are where w·x + b equals +1 and −1. So a point is on its own side and outside the street when y × (w·x + b) is at least 1. One test works for both classes because the labels are −1 and +1.

    As a check, the page tried every tilt in steps of 0.01°. None gives a wider empty street.

  4. What moves the line

    Now move two of the points yourself.

    Look at the dashed rings: drag triangles A and B, or use Tab and the arrow keys.

    A is not a support vector. Move it anywhere outside the street on its own side: "line moved" stays "no". Any of the 21 points that are not support vectors would act the same.

    B is a support vector. Move it towards or away from the line and the line follows; the dotted line marks where it was. Drag a point across the line, among the other class, and no empty street may exist. The page then lets points into the street (step 5), and "margin violations" counts them. "Put A and B back" resets both.

    The maths

    The problem can be rewritten with one number αi≥0\alpha_i \ge 0αi​≥0 per point. This second form is called the dual. Its answer gives

    w=∑i=1nαi yi xi,∑i=1nαi yi=0\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i\, y_i\, \mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i\, y_i = 0w=i=1∑n​αi​yi​xi​,i=1∑n​αi​yi​=0

    and αi\alpha_iαi​ is 0 for every point off the edges of the street (in the hard margin; with the soft margin of step 5, points inside the street have αi=C\alpha_i = Cαi​=C). Only the support vectors have αi>0\alpha_i > 0αi​>0, so only they appear in w\mathbf{w}w and bbb. The αi\alpha_iαi​ maximise

    ∑iαi−12∑i∑jαiαj yiyj xi⋅xjsubject to0≤αi≤C,  ∑iαiyi=0\sum_{i} \alpha_i - \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j} \alpha_i \alpha_j\, y_i y_j\, \mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j \quad \text{subject to} \quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C,\ \ \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0i∑​αi​−21​i∑​j∑​αi​αj​yi​yj​xi​⋅xj​subject to0≤αi​≤C,  i∑​αi​yi​=0

    with CCC from the next step (for the hard margin there is no upper limit). The page solves it in your browser by sequential minimal optimisation, which improves two αi\alpha_iαi​ at a time, and checks the answer against the conditions for the best answer to within 10−610^{-6}10−6.

    Steps 3 and 4 use CCC = 1. On the original points the largest αi\alpha_iαi​ is 0.15, well below that limit, so the answer is exactly the hard-margin one. With CCC = 1 the page may let a point into the street even when some line could still split the classes. The read-out's street width is then 2/∥w∥2/\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert2/∥w∥, the width between the dashed edges, which some points sit inside.

    The page solves the problem again after every move. Sliding B along its edge of the street may leave the line where it was. When A moves into the street, other points can stop being support vectors, so watch the count. A moved point that only enters the street becomes a support vector on the new street's edge, so the street stays empty. If it crosses the line, no empty street may exist; the page then lets points sit inside the street, as step 5 explains, and the read-out counts each one as a margin violation.

  5. Overlap and C

    Next, new points whose classes overlap.

    Look at the 12 circles and 12 triangles: no straight line splits them. A soft margin lets some into the street: a point inside it or on the "wrong side" is a margin violation and pays a charge, its hinge loss, summed as "total hinge". Rings mark violations and points on an edge, the support vectors.

    The SVM trades street width against total charge; the "C" slider sets the price. At its left end, C = 0.01, violations are cheap: a street 6.66 wide, with 22. At its right end, C = 100, they are dear: 1.59 wide, with 4.

    Note: a large C can chase odd training points, then do worse on new points than on these. That gap is overfitting. Pick C on held-out points, as in Train, validate, test.

    The maths

    The soft margin gives each point a slack ξi≥0\xi_i \ge 0ξi​≥0, how far it is allowed past its edge of the street:

    min⁡w, b, ξ 12∥w∥2+C∑i=1nξisubject toyi(w⋅xi+b)≥1−ξi,  ξi≥0\min_{\mathbf{w},\, b,\, \boldsymbol{\xi}}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{n} \xi_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0w,b,ξmin​ 21​∥w∥2+Ci=1∑n​ξi​subject toyi​(w⋅xi​+b)≥1−ξi​,  ξi​≥0

    At the best answer each slack is the hinge loss

    ξi=max⁡(0, 1−yi(w⋅xi+b))\xi_i = \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b)\big)ξi​=max(0, 1−yi​(w⋅xi​+b))

    The margin is still 2/∥w∥2/\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert2/∥w∥, but now some points sit inside the street. As CCC grows, the street can only narrow or stay the same, on any data. Compare the best answers for C1<C2C_1 < C_2C1​<C2​: each is at least as good as the other under its own CCC, and adding the two statements shows that the larger CCC has no more total hinge loss and no shorter w\mathbf{w}w. Here the street stops changing above C = 10. The αi\alpha_iαi​ still grow with CCC, but their changes cancel out in w\mathbf{w}w and bbb.

    The labels yi=±1y_i = \pm 1yi​=±1 make every condition one inequality: yi(w⋅xi+b)y_i(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i + b)yi​(w⋅xi​+b) is positive exactly when the point is on its own side of the line.

    The hinge loss is 0 for a point on its own edge of the street or beyond it, and grows in a straight line the further the point goes in. A point on the line itself is charged 1, and a point past the line more than 1. The read-out's "total hinge" adds them up. At every C on the slider at least one point stays on the wrong side, because the classes overlap. The slider starts at C = 1, where the street is 1.89 wide with 6 margin violations. These points are more spread out than those in steps 1 to 4.

  6. Curved boundaries

    Now no straight line will do.

    Look at the left plot: 20 circles in the middle, 20 triangles round them. The SVM's line gets 30 of the 40 training points right.

    Press "Add x₁² + x₂²". Each point gets a third feature, its squared distance from the middle, the height on the right. Side-on, the SVM's cut, a plane in three dimensions, is a line with every point on its own side.

    On the left, that cut is the solid circle, with its street between the dashed ones. Only the features changed: a kernel is a shortcut that gives the same SVM such features without writing them down.

    The next lesson, Decision trees, sorts the classes with yes/no questions instead.

    The maths

    With features (x1, x2, x12+x22)(x_1,\, x_2,\, x_1^2 + x_2^2)(x1​,x2​,x12​+x22​) the boundary is

    w1x1+w2x2+w3 (x12+x22)+b=0w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3\,(x_1^2 + x_2^2) + b = 0w1​x1​+w2​x2​+w3​(x12​+x22​)+b=0

    Here w3w_3w3​ = 1.11. Because x12x_1^2x12​ and x22x_2^2x22​ have the same weight, completing the square turns this into a circle, centred at −(w1,w2)/(2w3)-(w_1, w_2)/(2 w_3)−(w1​,w2​)/(2w3​). The street's edges, where the left side equals ±1\pm 1±1, are circles with the same centre.

    The dual in step 4 uses the points only through dot products xi⋅xj\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_jxi​⋅xj​. Swap each one for K(xi,xj)K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)K(xi​,xj​), the dot product of the two points after a change of features, worked out straight from the original features. That is the kernel trick: the SVM learns with the new features without ever writing them down. A common choice is the radial basis function (RBF) kernel, which scores two points by how close they are. Its new features are infinitely many, so it is used through the kernel trick. This page writes its one new feature out in full and uses no kernel.

    In numbers: the circles lie within distance 1 of the middle, so their new feature is below 1, and every triangle's is above 2.56. The straight line on the left is the SVM's answer with C = 1, right on 75.0% of the points. With the new feature the street is 1.79 wide, measured in three dimensions, and back on the flat picture the boundary is a circle of radius 1.35.

Quick check

Removing a point that is not a support vector does what?

Taking a point away cannot make the street narrower: the old street is still empty. And this point was not touching the street, so the widest street stays the same.

Right. Its αi\alpha_iαi​ is 0, so it plays no part in w or b. In step 3, removing any one of the 21 points off the edges leaves the answer exactly as it was. Moving point A in step 4 shows the same thing.

That is how a mean behaves, where every point pulls a little. The SVM line depends only on the support vectors.

Every point is checked while solving, but only the support vectors end up with αi>0\alpha_i > 0αi​>0, and only they appear in w and b.

Small C means…

That is large C. When violations are dear, the SVM narrows the street to keep points out of it.

C changes the street, not its shape: the boundary stays straight. A curve needs new features, as in step 6.

Right. Small C makes violations cheap, so a wide street wins. In step 5, C = 0.01 gives a street 6.66 wide with 22 margin violations; C = 100 gives 1.59 with 4.

That is the hard margin, which very large C approaches when some line separates the classes. Small C is the other end.

Lesson complete

  • An SVM picks the line w·x + b = 0 with the widest street around it. The street's width, the margin, is 2/‖w‖.
  • Only the support vectors, the points on the edges, inside the street or on the wrong side, fix the line. The other points could move or vanish without changing it.
  • When the classes overlap, a soft margin lets points into the street. Small C gives a wide street with many violations; large C a narrow one with few.
  • A new feature such as x₁² + x₂² lets the same straight-line method draw a curved boundary. The kernel trick does this without writing the features down.

Common misconceptions

  • "An SVM uses every training point to place the line." Every point is checked, but only the support vectors decide where the line goes.
  • "A larger C is better, because it makes fewer mistakes on the training points." Fewer training mistakes can mean a street bent around odd points. Choose C on data the model has not seen.
  • "The kernel is what makes it an SVM." The straight-line SVM is complete on its own. Kernels are a way to give it new features.
