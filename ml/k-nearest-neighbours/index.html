k-nearest neighbours

To label a new case, look at the cases most like it and let them vote.

About 10 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

So far every model has predicted a number. This lesson predicts a label instead: which of two classes a case belongs to.

The method is about as simple as machine learning gets. Keep the training cases, and when a new case arrives, let the k closest ones vote. We watch the vote, shade the whole plot by its answer, turn k up and down, and then change the units of one feature to see why that matters.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • how k-nearest neighbours (k-NN) labels a new case, and what accuracy measures
  • why k = 1 is perfect on the training data and why that means little, and how to choose k
  • why the units of the features change k-NN's answer, and how standardising fixes that

  2. A new case: ask the neighbours

    Find the k training cases closest to the new case and let them vote. The majority wins.

    The hollow diamond is a new case with no label. Drag it, or tab to it and use the arrow keys.

    Here k = 3. Lines join the new case to its 3 nearest training cases, which are ringed. The dashed circle passes through the third nearest; the other two lie inside it.

    At the start, at feature 1 = and feature 2 = , the vote is circles to triangle, so k-NN predicts class .

    Closest means the ordinary straight-line distance on the plot, the one Lesson 2 used to compare fruit. With two classes and an odd k, the vote can never be a tie.

    There is no training step. The model is the training data itself, kept until a new case arrives.

    The maths

    The distance from a new case x\mathbf{x}x to training case xi\mathbf{x}_ixi​ is

    ∥x−xi∥=(x1−xi1)2+(x2−xi2)2\lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i \rVert = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_{i1})^2 + (x_2 - x_{i2})^2}∥x−xi​∥=(x1​−xi1​)2+(x2​−xi2​)2​

    Let Nk(x)N_k(\mathbf{x})Nk​(x) be the kkk training cases with the smallest distances. If two are exactly as far away, the one made first wins the place. The vote is a majority:

    y^={1if ∑i∈Nk(x)yi>k/20otherwise\hat{y} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} y_i > k/2 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}y^​={10​if ∑i∈Nk​(x)​yi​>k/2otherwise​

    The sum counts the class-1 votes. With odd kkk it can never equal k/2k/2k/2, so there is no tie. With more than two classes, the most common class among the kkk wins, and ties can happen even for odd kkk (three neighbours from three classes), so a tie rule is needed.

    The cost sits at prediction time: each new case needs its distance to all nnn training cases, so twice the training data means twice as many distances to compute for every prediction.

What it does promise is that the units someone happened to record in do not decide the answer.

Quick check

What does k = 1 give on the training data?

Right. Each training case is its own nearest neighbour, at distance 0, so it always gets its own label back. On new cases k = 1 had the lowest test accuracy of any k on the slider.

Here k = 1 had the lowest test accuracy of any k on the slider. The single closest case can be one that sits among the other class by chance.

The one vote is not random: on a training case it comes from the case itself, so it is always right there.

The opposite. k = 1 gives the most jagged boundary, with islands around single cases. Larger k smooths it.

Why standardise the features before k-NN?

k-NN has no training step to speed up; it just keeps the training cases. Standardising changes how distance is measured.

Right. Distance adds up differences in every feature, so a feature measured in large numbers swamps the rest. z-scores put every feature on the same footing, and the same data in other units gives the same predictions.

Not always. On this data the original units did slightly better ( against ). The two features already had spreads of a similar size, so standardising changed little, and here it happened to cost a few cases.

It shifts and rescales each feature, nothing more. An unusual case stays unusual; it just gets a large z-score.

Lesson complete

  • Classification predicts a label. Accuracy is the fraction of cases labelled correctly, and the one that counts is on unseen cases.
  • k-NN labels a new case by a majority vote of its k nearest training cases. An odd k avoids ties with two classes.
  • k = 1 is 100% right on the training data and overfits. A larger k smooths the boundary; too large a k underfits. Choose k on a validation set.
  • Distances depend on units. Standardise the features, using the training mean and standard deviation, before k-NN.

Common misconceptions

  • "k-NN learns a rule from the data." It stores the training cases; all the work happens when a new case arrives.
  • "100% training accuracy means a great model." With k = 1 it comes almost for free, whatever the data (the only exception is two identical cases with different labels).
  • "Standardising always improves accuracy." It removes the effect of the units. Whether the score goes up depends on the data.

About the data. The cases are made up. Each class is a round cloud: feature 1 = m₁ + 1.1 z₁ and feature 2 = m₂ + 1.1 z₂, where z₁ and z₂ are independent draws from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1, made with mulberry32. Class 0 has centre (m₁, m₂) = (2, 2) and class 1 has centre (4, 3.4). The 40 training cases (20 per class, class 0 first) use seed 41; the 300 test cases (150 per class) use seed 42. The starting spot of the new case, (3.5, 2.5), was chosen so the vote there is not one-sided. Distances are straight-line distances; when two training cases are exactly as far away, the one made first counts as nearer. Standardising uses the training cases' mean and population SD. Displayed values are rounded; the calculations are not.
