The artificial neuron

A weighted sum, a bias and a squashing curve: the building block of every neural network, and an old friend in disguise.

About 10 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

Neural networks are built from one small part, repeated many times: the artificial neuron. This lesson takes one neuron apart. It adds up its inputs with weights, adds a bias, and passes the total through a curve called an activation.

With the sigmoid as its activation, one neuron is exactly the logistic regression of Level 3. That tells us what it can do, and what it cannot: its boundary is a straight line. On data arranged like a two-by-two chessboard, no straight line will do, and that is why networks have more than one neuron.

The data are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • what a neuron computes: a weighted sum plus a bias, passed through an activation
  • why a single sigmoid neuron is logistic regression, so its boundary is a straight line
  • why one neuron cannot split XOR data, and how two lines can

  1. Inputs, weights, sum

    A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up and adds a bias.

    Look at the plot: 60 made-up points with two features, x₁ across and x₂ up. Cyan circles are class 0, violet triangles class 1.

    The diagram on the right is one neuron. Its inputs are the ringed point's x₁ = 1.14 and x₂ = 0.63, and each link has a weight, w₁ or w₂. The total, plus a bias b (a fixed number added at the end), is z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b.

    At the start, w₁ = 1.00, w₂ = −0.50 and b = 0.50, so z = 1.32. Move the sliders to change it. Thicker links mean larger weights; dashed violet ones, negative.

    Note: "neuron" is a loose analogy with nerve cells; none of the maths uses it.

    The maths

    Collect the inputs into a vector x=(x1,x2)\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2)x=(x1​,x2​) and the weights into w=(w1,w2)\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2)w=(w1​,w2​). The weighted sum is a dot product:

    z=w⋅x+b=w1x1+w2x2+bz = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + bz=w⋅x+b=w1​x1​+w2​x2​+b

    This is the same straight-line score as logistic regression and linear regression with many features. The bias bbb plays the part of the intercept: it shifts zzz up or down for every point at once.

  2. The activation curve

    Next, z goes through a curve g, the activation. The output is a = g(z).

    Look at the plot: the curve of the activation picked with the buttons (step, sigmoid, tanh, ReLU), with the ringed point's z marked across and its output a marked up.

    At the starting z = 1.32, the step gives 1.00, the sigmoid 0.79, tanh 0.87 and ReLU 1.32.

    • Step: 1 if z > 0, otherwise 0.
    • Sigmoid σ: a smooth S from 0 to 1.
    • tanh: the same S, from −1 to 1.
    • ReLU: z if z > 0, otherwise 0.

    The read-out also gives the curve's slope at z. Later, training follows slopes to adjust the weights. The step's slope is 0 except at the jump, where it has none, so it gives training nothing to follow.

  3. It is logistic regression

    From here on the neuron uses the sigmoid, so its output σ(z) is exactly logistic regression's p^\hat{p}p^​.

    Look at the read-out: the ringed point's p^\hat{p}p^​ (the probability of class 1) and the accuracy on the 60 points. On the solid line, p^\hat{p}p^​ = 0.5.

    Press "Fit". The sliders jump to the weights logistic regression finds for these points, those with the smallest log-loss (Level 3's error score): w₁ = 2.57, w₂ = 3.48, b = 1.13. Calling class 1 wherever p^\hat{p}p^​ is 0.5 or more gets 95.0% of these points right.

    With the same weights, the neuron's output and logistic regression's p^\hat{p}p^​ agree for every point to within rounding error (10⁻¹⁵). They are the same calculation.

    The maths
    a=σ(w⋅x+b)=p^a = \sigma(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b) = \hat{p}a=σ(w⋅x+b)=p^​

    σ here is the sigmoid, not a standard deviation. Fitting chooses w\mathbf{w}w and bbb to make the mean log-loss as small as possible. In deep learning the same loss is called binary cross-entropy:

    L=−1n∑i=1n[ yiln⁡ai+(1−yi)ln⁡(1−ai)]\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[\, y_i \ln a_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - a_i) \Big]L=−n1​i=1∑n​[yi​lnai​+(1−yi​)ln(1−ai​)]

    Here L\mathcal{L}L is the mean over all nnn points. The Fit button uses Newton's method, as in Level 3. Gradient descent would approach the same weights, because this loss is convex.

  4. One neuron, one straight line

    Whatever the weights, the neuron splits the plane with a straight line.

    Look at the shading: the neuron's output σ(z) at each spot, violet near 1, cyan near 0. The solid line is the boundary, where z = 0.

    Move the sliders: the line turns and slides, but never bends. The sigmoid curves the output, not the boundary. w₁ and w₂ set the line's direction; b shifts it. With w₁ = w₂ = 0 there is no line: z = b everywhere.

    Why? The output depends on a point only through z, and points with equal z lie on a straight line. All four activations of step 2 only rise or stay flat as z rises, so the z > 0 side always gets the higher output: one straight boundary.

    The maths

    The boundary is the set

    { x:w⋅x+b=0 }\{\, \mathbf{x} : \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0 \,\}{x:w⋅x+b=0}

    which, for w≠0\mathbf{w} \neq \mathbf{0}w=0, is a straight line at right angles to w\mathbf{w}w. Every level set w⋅x+b=c\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = cw⋅x+b=c is a line parallel to it, so the output is constant along lines parallel to the boundary.

    The shading is worked out on a grid of 54 × 50 small squares, each coloured by σ(z) at its centre. The line is drawn exactly.

  5. XOR: where one line fails

    New data, where no straight line works.

    Look at the plot: 80 made-up points in four groups near the corners (±1, ±1). Class 1 is top left and bottom right, where the corner's signs differ. This is XOR, "exclusive or".

    Move the sliders: some group always lands on the wrong side. Even the four corners alone cannot be split by a line.

    The page tried 360 directions, each with every cut between points. The best ("best line"; "this line" is yours) gets 77.5% of the 80 points right. A full check of every possible line finds none better.

    Note: 42 of the 80 are class 1, so calling everything class 1 already gets 52.5%. The best line beats that, but still misses 18.

    The maths

    Take the four corners alone. A line would need z>0z > 0z>0 at (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) and (−1,1)(-1, 1)(−1,1) and z<0z < 0z<0 at (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) and (−1,−1)(-1, -1)(−1,−1):

    w1−w2+b>0,−w1+w2+b>0,w1+w2+b<0,−w1−w2+b<0w_1 - w_2 + b > 0, \quad -w_1 + w_2 + b > 0, \quad w_1 + w_2 + b < 0, \quad -w_1 - w_2 + b < 0w1​−w2​+b>0,−w1​+w2​+b>0,w1​+w2​+b<0,−w1​−w2​+b<0

    Adding the first two gives 2b>02b > 02b>0; adding the last two gives 2b<02b < 02b<0. Both cannot hold, so no weights work. The noise around each corner does not rescue it.

    A second check tries every way a straight line can split these 80 points, with class 1 on either side, and finds the same 77.5%. Why it is complete: the order of the points along a direction only changes at directions where two points line up, so trying one direction between each such pair covers every possible split.

  6. Two neurons, two lines

    Two neurons can draw two lines, and a third can combine them.

    Look at the diagram: three step neurons, set by hand. Nothing is trained.

    The first hidden neuron fires (outputs 1) when x₁ − x₂ > 1, below dashed line 1; the second when x₂ − x₁ > 1, above line 2. The output neuron fires when either does.

    The band between the lines is class 0, the rest class 1: "two lines" gets 98.8% of the 80 points right, "best one line" 77.5%.

    These two middle neurons form a hidden layer. In the next lesson, Hidden layers, a network learns where to put its lines by lowering its loss on the training data, and the boundary bends.

    The maths

    With the step function as ggg, the hand-set network is

    h1=g(x1−x2−1),h2=g(−x1+x2−1),output=g(h1+h2−0.5)h_1 = g(x_1 - x_2 - 1), \qquad h_2 = g(-x_1 + x_2 - 1), \qquad \text{output} = g(h_1 + h_2 - 0.5)h1​=g(x1​−x2​−1),h2​=g(−x1​+x2​−1),output=g(h1​+h2​−0.5)

    The two hidden neurons have weights (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) and (−1,1)(-1, 1)(−1,1), both with bias −1-1−1; the output neuron has weights (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) and bias −0.5-0.5−0.5. The diagram shows them: solid links positive, dashed negative.

Quick check

What does a single sigmoid neuron compute?

Right. a = σ(w·x + b) is the logistic regression model. With the same weights the two agree to within rounding error.

The sigmoid bends the output, not the boundary. The boundary is where z = 0, and z is a straight-line score, so the boundary is a straight line.

The weighted sum is z. The sigmoid then squashes it into a number between 0 and 1.

The name comes from a loose analogy, but the neuron is a short formula: a weighted sum, a bias and an activation.

Why can no single neuron solve XOR?

Right. On these data the best line of all gets 77.5% right. The corners alone already rule a line out: the conditions for the four corners contradict each other.

The output depends on the point only through z, and ReLU, like the sigmoid, only rises or stays flat as z rises. With any of the four activations here, the neuron's regions are split by one straight line.

More points in the same four groups would not help. Even the four corners on their own cannot be split by a line.

The page checked every possible line, not just one found by training. None does better than 77.5%.

Lesson complete

  • A neuron computes z = w·x + b and outputs a = g(z), where g is its activation: step, sigmoid, tanh or ReLU.
  • With a sigmoid, one neuron is exactly logistic regression, and fitting it means making the log-loss (binary cross-entropy) small.
  • With any of the four activations here (all only rise or stay flat as z rises), one neuron's boundary is a straight line, so it cannot split XOR data.
  • Two neurons can draw two lines and a third can combine them. Learning where to put the lines is the next lesson.

Common misconceptions

  • "An artificial neuron works like a brain cell." The name is a loose analogy. The neuron is a weighted sum, a bias and an activation.
  • "A different activation would let one neuron bend the boundary." The output depends on the point only through z, and step, sigmoid, tanh and ReLU only rise or stay flat as z rises, so with any of them the boundary stays one straight line.
  • "The sigmoid makes the boundary curved." It curves the output along the direction of w; the boundary z = 0 is still a line.

About the data. All the data are made up, in your browser, by the mulberry32 random number generator. The 60 points in steps 1 to 4 use seed 143: x₁ = m₁ + 0.8 z₁ and x₂ = m₂ + 0.8 z₂, where z₁ and z₂ are independent draws from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1. Class 0 has centre (m₁, m₂) = (−1, −1) and class 1 has centre (1.2, 1), with 30 points each, class 0 first; the ringed point is the second point of class 1. The 80 XOR points in steps 5 and 6 use seed 142: for each point a corner (±1, ±1) is chosen at random with equal chances, then x₁ and x₂ are the corner's coordinates plus 0.35 times independent normal draws with mean 0 and SD 1. A point is class 1 when the corner's two signs differ.
