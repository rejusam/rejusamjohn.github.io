Gradient descent

Gradient descent finds the bottom of a loss by taking small steps downhill, again and again.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

Lesson 6 found the best line with a formula. Most models have no such formula. They are trained by gradient descent instead: start somewhere, look at the slope of the loss, take a small step downhill, and repeat.

This lesson uses the same 25 points as Lesson 6, so you can check that walking downhill ends at the same line the formula gave.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • how the slope of the loss tells the model which way to move, and how far
  • why the steps get shorter near the bottom on their own
  • what the learning rate does when it is too small or too big
  • why a bowl-shaped loss is easy, and what can go wrong on a bumpy one

  1. One number to learn

    To see gradient descent clearly, we give the model just one number to learn: the slope of a line, called w. Every w has a loss, and together they make a curve.

    We take Lesson 6's points and centre them: subtract the mean of x from every x, and the mean of y from every y. The cloud keeps its shape, but its centre moves to (0, 0).

    Lesson 6 showed that the least-squares line passes through the point of means. After centring, that point is (0, 0), so the best line goes through it. Only its slope is left to find. The model is ŷ = w·x.

    Each w gives one line, drawn on the right, and one loss: its mean squared error (MSE) from Lesson 4. Plot the loss for every w and you get the curve L(w). Move the slider to try different values of w.

    The curve is a bowl with one lowest point. Lesson 4 said the squared loss is smooth, so the curve has a slope at every w.

    Learning one number is a simplification for this lesson. Real models learn many numbers at once; Lesson 8 learns two.

  2. The slope says which way is downhill

    At the current w, the slope of the loss curve says which way is downhill. Move w the opposite way to the slope's sign.

    The violet line touches the curve at the ball. Its slope is the slope of the loss at that w, written L′(w).

    Left of the bottom, the slope is negative, so downhill is to the right. Right of the bottom, it is positive, so downhill is to the left. This is the rule from Lesson 5: the sign of the slope tells you which way is downhill.

    The slope also says how steep the curve is. Far from the bottom it is large. Near the bottom it is close to 0. Move the slider and watch both change.

  3. Take a step downhill

    The slope tells the model which way is downhill. It moves its weight a little in that direction, then checks the slope again.

    Each step replaces w with w − η × slope. The learning rate η (the Greek letter eta) is a small positive number that sets the size of the steps.

    Subtracting η × slope moves w against the slope's sign, which is downhill. The size of the move is η times how steep the curve is.

  5. Too small, too big

    The learning rate decides whether the walk settles. Too small, and it crawls. Too big, and it overshoots the bottom; bigger still, and the overshoots grow.

    The slider runs on a log scale, from η = to : moving it the same distance always multiplies η by the same amount. The left chart and the right chart both show the first steps.

    • Up to η = : steady. w moves towards the bottom from one side. At the low end it is slow, and steps cover only part of the way.
    • Between and : zig-zags but settles. Each step jumps past the bottom, but by less each time.
    • At exactly : never settles. w jumps back and forth between two points at the same distance from the bottom, for ever. A slider almost never lands on this exact value.
    • Above : diverges. Each jump lands further away than the last, and w runs off the chart. The ball is held at the edge and the page says so.

    The boundaries come from the data: the more spread out x is, the steeper the bowl and the smaller η must be. With other data they move.

  6. One bottom, or many dips?

    On a bowl-shaped loss like this one, walking downhill leads to the lowest point. On a bumpy loss it can stop in a dip that is not the lowest.

    A curve shaped like a bowl, one that never bends downwards, is called convex. On a convex curve, any bottom you reach is the lowest point of all, the global minimum. This lesson's loss is convex, so with η in the stable range gradient descent closes in on w* from any starting w.

    The small curve on the right is a different, made-up curve, not this lesson's loss. It has two dips. Started on the right, gradient descent walks downhill into the nearer dip and stops there, because the slope is 0 at its bottom. It never finds the lower dip on the left.

    A dip that is lowest only in its own neighbourhood is called a local minimum. The losses of neural networks (deep learning) are usually not bowl-shaped, so there gradient descent can stop in a local dip rather than at the lowest point.

Quick check

What happens if the learning rate η is too large?

Bigger steps help only up to a point. Past it, w jumps past the bottom, and past a second point it never settles at all.

Right. In step 5, η above the first boundary makes w zig-zag past the bottom, and η above the second makes each jump bigger than the last.

Local dips come from the shape of the loss, not from η. This lesson's loss is a bowl with no local dips, yet a large η still fails.

The step is η times the slope, so η scales every step. The slope sets the direction and steepness; η sets how far to go.

With η fixed, why do the steps get shorter near the bottom?

In this lesson η never changes. Some training methods do lower η on a schedule, but the shrinking here happens without that.

Right. The step is η × slope. As the bowl flattens towards its bottom, the slope shrinks, and so does the step.

It is the other way round. The bowl is steepest far from the bottom and flat at the bottom itself.

Every step here uses all 25 points. The steps shrink because the slope does.

Lesson complete

  • Gradient descent repeats one move: w becomes w − η × slope. The slope's sign gives the direction, downhill.
  • With η fixed, the steps shrink near the bottom because the slope does. On these centred points it finds the same slope as least squares, and so Lesson 6's line.
  • Too small an η is slow. Too large, and w zig-zags; larger still, and it runs away. On a bowl it ends at the lowest point; on a bumpy loss it can stop in a local dip.

Common misconceptions

  • "A bigger learning rate always learns faster." Only up to a point. Past it the steps overshoot, and further still they grow without end.
  • "The model slows down on purpose near the bottom." Here η stays fixed. The steps shrink because the slope shrinks.
  • "Gradient descent always finds the best answer." On a convex loss with a stable η it closes in on it. On a bumpy loss it can stop in a local dip.
