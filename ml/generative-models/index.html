Generative models

A generative model learns where the data falls, as a probability distribution, and then makes new data by sampling from it. Fit one live with EM and press Sample.

About 10 minutes · Every fit, sample and score is computed in your browser; the formulas are in The maths

Overview

The last lesson, Large language models, in miniature, made new text by sampling one character at a time from learned probabilities. That is one kind of generative model. This lesson shows the idea on points, where every step can be drawn: describe the data with a probability distribution, fit it by maximum likelihood, and sample from it.

The model is a mixture of Gaussians, fitted with the EM algorithm. It is small enough to watch, and it shows the questions every generative model faces: how good is the fit, how many parts does it need, and what can it not describe?

You'll learn

  • what a generative model is, and how fitting one means maximising the likelihood of the data
  • how EM fits a mixture of Gaussians, and why held-out data chooses the number of components
  • how to sample new points, and where a few Gaussians are not enough

  1. Making more data

    A classifier predicts a label for a point. A generative model describes where the points fall, so it can make more of them.

    Look at the two panels. Left: "the data", 300 training points. Right: 300 new points drawn from a model fitted to them. None of the new points is in the data.

    A classifier, such as logistic regression, is given a point and predicts its label. It says nothing about which points are likely in the first place.

    A generative model learns that: a probability distribution p(x) over the points themselves. From it, it can draw new points.

    The maths

    A classifier models p(y∣x)p(y \mid \mathbf{x})p(y∣x): the label given the point. A generative model learns p(x)p(\mathbf{x})p(x), or p(x,y)p(\mathbf{x}, y)p(x,y) when there are labels. Naive Bayes was in fact generative: it modelled p(x∣y)p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)p(x∣y) for each class and used Bayes' rule to classify. With p(x)p(\mathbf{x})p(x) you can sample new points, and score how likely any point is.

  2. One Gaussian, by maximum likelihood

    Start with the simplest generative model: one bell curve, called a Gaussian.

    Look at the bars: "the 300 values", in two humps. The solid line, "one Gaussian, fitted", is the Gaussian under which they are most likely. Its mean is 0.36 and its population variance (spread) 2.39.

    It peaks between the humps, where values are few. Press "Sample": 300 values drawn from it (dashed) make one hump, not two.

    The score is the mean log-likelihood: the average log of the curve's height at held-out values: new ones from the two-hump rule, never fitted. Higher is better: one Gaussian scores −1.91, the rule −1.64.

    The maths

    The likelihood of parameters θ\thetaθ is the density of the data under them, the values taken as independent: L(θ)=∏i=1np(xi∣θ)L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid \theta)L(θ)=∏i=1n​p(xi​∣θ).     μ^=1n∑i=1nxi,σ^2=1n∑i=1n(xi−μ^)2,\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \hat{\mu})^2,μ^​=n1​i=1∑n​xi​,σ^2=n1​i=1∑n​(xi​−μ^​)2,

    the mean and the population variance (n, not n − 1). The scores on this page are the mean log-likelihood per point, ℓ/n\ell/nℓ/n, with natural logs. A density can be above 1, so a log-likelihood can have either sign; only compare scores on the same points.

    On the held-out values the two-hump rule scores 0.27 higher than the one Gaussian.

  3. A mixture of Gaussians

    One Gaussian makes one hump. A mixture adds several, each with a weight.

    Look at the 300 training points of step 1, made by three Gaussians, a third of the points each. Each Gaussian is drawn as an ellipse holding about 86% of its points.

    The solid ellipse, "one Gaussian, fitted", also covers the gaps between groups: mean log-likelihood −3.93. As a mixture, the three dashed Gaussians that made the data score higher: −3.08.

    Each of a mixture's K components has a mean μk (centre), a covariance Σk (ellipse size, shape and tilt) and a weight πk, its share of points (π is not 3.14… here).

    The maths
    p(x)=∑k=1Kπk N(x∣μk,Σk),πk≥0,∑k=1Kπk=1.p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k), \qquad \pi_k \ge 0, \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1.p(x)=k=1∑K​πk​N(x∣μk​,Σk​),πk​≥0,k=1∑K​πk​=1.

    To sample one point: pick component k with probability πk\pi_kπk​, then draw from its Gaussian. The ellipse is the set of x\mathbf{x}x with (x−μk)⊤Σk−1(x−μk)=22(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k) = 2^2(x−μk​)⊤Σk−1​(x−μk​)=22; in two dimensions a Gaussian puts 1−e−2≈0.861 - e^{-2} \approx 0.861−e−2≈0.86 of its probability inside it.

    Each ellipse on the page marks the points at Mahalanobis distance 2 from its mean.

  4. Fitting with EM

    Now press "Train" to fit K = 3 with EM.

    Look at the points: each takes the shape of the component (ellipse) likeliest to have made it. The curve is the mean log-likelihood after each round (EM iteration).

    EM starts from three random training points as centres (equal odds) and weights 1/3. Each round, the E-step gives every point its responsibilities: the chance each component made it. The M-step refits each Gaussian, weighting points by them.

    After the run, the score has risen from −4.37 to −3.05 in 29 rounds, never falling.

    Note: like k-means, EM can stop at a local maximum; another start can end in a worse fit.

    The maths

    E-step, the responsibility of component k for point i:

    rik=πk N(xi∣μk,Σk)∑j=1Kπj N(xi∣μj,Σj).r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)}.rik​=∑j=1K​πj​N(xi​∣μj​,Σj​)πk​N(xi​∣μk​,Σk​)​.

    M-step, with Nk=∑irikN_k = \sum_i r_{ik}Nk​=∑i​rik​:

    πk=Nkn,μk=1Nk∑i=1nrik xi,Σk=1Nk∑i=1nrik (xi−μk)(xi−μk)⊤.\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ik}\,\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ik}\,(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^{\top}.πk​=nNk​​,μk​=Nk​1​i=1∑n​rik​xi​,Σk​=Nk​1​i=1∑n​rik​(xi​−μk​)(xi​−μk​)⊤.

    No iteration can lower the log-likelihood ∑ilog⁡p(xi)\sum_i \log p(\mathbf{x}_i)∑i​logp(xi​); Dempster, Laird and Rubin (1977) proved this for EM in general. It does not promise the highest possible value, only a value that stops rising. Nothing is added to the covariances here; if a component shrank onto a single point its covariance would become singular, which does not happen in any run on this page.

    Points fade when they are shared between components. The run starts with every covariance equal to the whole data's sample covariance (dividing by n − 1). It stops when one iteration raises the mean log-likelihood by less than 0.000001. On the way the curve rises fast, nearly stalls for a few iterations, then climbs again as the ellipses separate the groups.

    EM is like k-means with soft assignments: k-means gives each point wholly to its nearest centre; EM shares it out by probability, and also learns each group's shape and weight.

  5. How many components?

    To choose K without knowing the rule, slide "components K" from 1 to 5.

    Look at the chart. "training" (solid, circles) scores the points EM fitted. "held-out" (dashed, triangles) scores 300 new points from the same rule, never fitted.

    The training score rises with each component: −3.93 at K = 1, −3.01 at K = 5. More components hug the training points.

    The held-out score is best at K = 3, −3.17, just under the rule's own −3.16. K = 4 and 5 are 0.02 lower: they fit quirks of the training points, as in overfitting.

    Note: K = 3 wins only just. Another set of held-out points can put K = 4 first.

    The maths

    A mixture of K Gaussians in two dimensions has 6K − 1 numbers to fit: 2 for each mean, 3 for each covariance, and K weights that add up to 1. A bigger mixture can always copy a smaller one, for example by splitting one component into two identical halves of its weight, so at their best fit more components can always score at least as well on the training points. The training score can even grow without limit if one component shrinks onto a single point. So the training score cannot choose K; held-out data, or a penalty for the number of parameters, can.

    Every K starts and stops as in step 4. For K = 1 to 5 the training scores are −3.93, −3.38, −3.05, −3.03 and −3.01; the held-out scores are −3.92, −3.43, −3.17, −3.19 and −3.19. Here the groups are well apart; when groups overlap, several K often score almost the same.

  6. Sampling, and its limit

    Press "Sample" to draw from each fit.

    Look at the dots: hollow are data, filled are new, from the K = 3 fit of step 4 (left) and a K = 5 fit to 400 spiral points (right).

    In the read-out, "new→data" is the mean distance from each new point to its nearest data point. Blobs: about 0.15, as for held-out points.

    Spiral: 0.15 to 0.18 over 20 draws, about three times the 0.05 of fresh points from its own rule: five broad ellipses blur the arms.

    Note: enough components could follow the spiral. Real generative models use neural networks at far larger scale.

Small means the new points cover the data. Over the first 20 draws, the blobs give new→data 0.14 to 0.16 and data→new 0.13 to 0.15, against 0.15 and 0.16 for held-out points. Quick check

What can a generative model do that a classifier such as logistic regression cannot?

Right. It learns p(x), where the points fall, so it can draw new ones. A classifier learns only the label given a point.

Not necessarily. A generative model can be used to classify, as Naive Bayes is, but it is not more accurate by being generative. Its extra ability is describing the points themselves.

Lesson complete

  • A generative model learns a probability distribution for the data, p(x), and makes new data by sampling from it.
  • Fitting means maximising the likelihood. For a mixture of Gaussians, EM does it by alternating responsibilities (E-step) and weighted refits (M-step); the likelihood never falls, but it can stop at a local maximum.
  • Choose the number of components on held-out data. A few Gaussians describe a few blobs well, but a curved shape needs many.

Common misconceptions

  • "A generative model copies the training points." A few Gaussians cannot copy: their samples fall near the data, not on it. A model with one tiny component per point could copy, which is overfitting again. Either way, samples hold nothing the model did not already learn.
  • "More components are always better." The training score rises, but here the held-out score is best at K = 3.
  • "EM finds the best fit." It finds a fit that further iterations barely improve; another start can end somewhere worse.

About the data. All the data is made in your browser by the mulberry32 random number generator, with Gaussian draws by the Box–Muller method. The 1-D values: for each of 300, one uniform draw u; if u < 0.4 the value is drawn from N(−1.5, 0.5²), otherwise from N(1.5, 0.75²); seed 301 for the training values and 306 for the held-out values. The 2-D blobs: point i is drawn from Gaussian i mod 3, with means (−2, −1), (2, −1) and (0, 2.2) and covariances [[0.5, 0.2], [0.2, 0.4]], [[0.4, −0.15], [−0.15, 0.6]] and [[0.7, 0], [0, 0.25]]; 300 training points from seed 302 and 300 held-out points from seed 303. EM starts from seed 304 for every fit. The spiral: 400 points from seed 305, 200 on each of two arms; along an arm the radius grows from 0.3 towards 3 over one turn, the second arm starts half a turn round from the first, and Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.1 is added to each coordinate; each axis is then standardised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (dividing by n); the fresh spiral is made the same way from seed 307. Samples start at seed 308 for the 1-D values, 309 for the blobs (also the right-hand panel of step 1) and 310 for the spiral, and each press of Sample adds 1 to the seed.
