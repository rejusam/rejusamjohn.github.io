Gradient boosting

Add small trees one at a time, each fitted to what the model still gets wrong.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

In bagging and random forests, many trees are grown side by side and their answers averaged. Boosting grows trees one after another instead. Each new tree is tiny, and its job is to fix what the trees before it still get wrong.

We use the wave data from Overfitting and regularisation, with more points. We start with one tiny tree, look at what it misses, add a second, then add hundreds and watch training and test error. We finish with the reason for the word "gradient".

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • how boosting builds a model round by round, each small tree fitted to the residuals of the model so far
  • why training error keeps falling while test error turns back up, and how the learning rate η and the number of rounds M work together
  • why the residuals are a gradient, which puts the "gradient" in gradient boosting

  1. One stump

    A forest grows its trees side by side. Boosting grows small trees one at a time, starting from a stump, the smallest tree.

    Look at the left plot: 40 training points (cyan dots) around a wave. The plainest model, F₀, predicts their mean y everywhere: the dotted line at 0.12.

    A stump asks one question, "is x below the split?", and predicts the mean y of each side. The best one (cyan, "one stump, F₁") splits at x = −0.01, the dashed line, with levels −0.66 and 0.65. Training MSE (mean squared error) falls from 0.553 to 0.144.

    Note: a stump can only say "low" or "high", so it cannot follow the wave. It is a weak learner: a very simple model.

  2. The residuals

    Now draw a stick from each point to the stump.

    Look at the white sticks on the left. Each is a residual, rᵢ = yᵢ − F₁(xᵢ): the point's y minus the prediction, positive above the model and negative below. Training MSE, 0.144, is their mean squared length.

    The right plot stands them on a line at 0. On the far right most sit below 0: the wave turns down there, but the stump stays flat.

    That pattern is something another stump can fit. Every stick counts in the next fit, but points the model already fits well have short sticks, so they pull less.

    The maths

    After m−1m - 1m−1 rounds, the residual of training point iii is

  3. The next stump

    Fit a second stump to those residuals, not to y.

    Look at the right plot: the new stump, h₂ (dashed violet), splits at x = 0.66. It adds 0.06 left of the split and −0.30 right of it, pulling the model down where the wave turns down.

    On the left, F₁ is dashed grey and F₂ = F₁ + h₂ is cyan. The read-out's "1 ×" means h₂ is added in full. Training MSE falls from 0.144 to 0.124.

    That is the whole method: fit a stump to the residuals, add it, work out the new residuals, and repeat. Each repeat is a round, and each stump depends on the ones before. The model is a sum of stumps, an additive model.

    The maths

    Round mmm fits a least-squares stump hmh_mhm​ to the pairs (xi,rmi)(x_i, r_{mi})(xi​,rmi​) and adds η times it:

    Fm(x)=Fm−1(x)+η hm(x)F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta\, h_m(x)Fm​(x)=Fm−1​(x)+ηhm​(x)

    After MMM rounds the model is the mean plus a sum of shrunken stumps:

    FM(x)=yˉ+η∑m=1Mhm(x)F_M(x) = \bar{y} + \eta \sum_{m=1}^{M} h_m(x)FM​(x)=yˉ​+ηm=1∑M​hm​(x)

  4. Many rounds

    Now add only a fraction of each stump.

    Each stump is now added at η (eta) = 0.1 times its size. η is the learning rate, or shrinkage, as in gradient descent.

    Slide "M", the number of rounds, up to 300 (the white dashed line on the chart). The staircase follows the wave more closely.

    The chart (log scale) shows training MSE (cyan) on the 40 training points and test MSE (violet, dashed) on 300 held-out test points (violet squares).

    At the start, M = 20, the read-out shows training MSE 0.124 and test MSE 0.131. Training MSE falls every round, and with these stumps and η between 0 and 2 it can never rise (see the maths).

    The maths

    Claim. With least-squares stumps and 0<η<20 < \eta < 20<η<2, the training MSE never rises from one round to the next.

    Write r\mathbf{r}r for the residuals at the nnn training points and h\mathbf{h}h for the new stump's values there. Each level is the mean of the residuals on its side, so on each side the residuals minus the level add up to 0.     ∥r−ηh∥2=∥r∥2−2η r⋅h+η2∥h∥2=∥r∥2−η(2−η)∥h∥2\lVert\mathbf{r} - \eta\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2 - 2\eta\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{h} + \eta^2\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2 - \eta(2 - \eta)\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2∥r−ηh∥2=∥r∥2−2ηr⋅h+η2∥h∥2=∥r∥2−η(2−η)∥h∥2

  5. When to stop

    Test error does not keep falling.

    Look at the violet ring: with η = 0.1, test MSE is lowest, 0.097, at M = 111. By M = 300 it is 0.110, while training MSE is 0.025.

    This growing gap between training and test error is overfitting: later stumps fit random scatter in the 40 training points, which new points do not share.

    Move the "η" slider: smaller η needs more rounds. η = 0.05 is lowest at M = 207, with about the same test MSE.

    Note: stopping at the lowest point is early stopping. The ring uses test points only to show the idea. In practice you pick M on a validation set, data held back from training, and keep the test set for a final check.

    The maths

    The test MSE of FMF_MFM​ is 1300∑j(yj−FM(xj))2\frac{1}{300}\sum_{j} (y_j - F_M(x_j))^23001​∑j​(yj​−FM​(xj​))2 over the 300 test points. The training and test curves are worked out once for each η, for every MMM from 0 to 300, so moving the M slider only looks values up.

    The lowest test MSE comes at M = 207 for η = 0.05, 111 for 0.1, 34 for 0.3 and 16 for 1. The three smaller values of η all get down to about the same test MSE, 0.097. η = 1 rushes: its lowest is 0.106, after only 16 rounds.

    A validation set, or cross-validation, which reuses the training data in turns, is how M is chosen in practice (see Train, validate, test). The test set is kept for one final check.

    The noise in this data has variance 0.252=0.06250.25^2 = 0.06250.252=0.0625. On average over new points, no model can get its MSE below that.

  6. Why the name?

    The word "gradient" comes from this: the residuals point downhill on the loss.

    Look at the right plot: the residuals after 10 rounds with η = 0.1.

    Score a prediction F with the loss ½(yᵢ − F)², half the squared error. Its gradient (slope) with respect to F is −(yᵢ − F).

    Downhill is minus the slope, yᵢ − F: exactly the residual (the read-out's rᵢ = −∂L/∂F). If F is below yᵢ, it is positive, so it says raise F.

    So each round is like a gradient descent step on the predictions. The next stump (dashed violet) stands in for that direction, known only at the 40 training points.

    The next lesson, k-means clustering, has no y at all: it finds groups in unlabelled points.

    The maths

    With the loss L(y,F)=12(y−F)2L(y, F) = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - F)^2L(y,F)=21​(y−F)2,

    −∂L∂F∣F=Fm−1(xi)=yi−Fm−1(xi)=rmi-\frac{\partial L}{\partial F}\bigg|_{F = F_{m-1}(x_i)} = y_i - F_{m-1}(x_i) = r_{mi}−∂F∂L​​F=Fm−1​(xi​)​=yi​−Fm−1​(xi​)=rmi​

    Treat the nnn predictions F(x1),…,F(xn)F(x_1), \dots, F(x_n)F(x1​),…,F(xn​) as the things being learned. The total loss is ∑iL(yi,F(xi))\sum_i L(y_i, F(x_i))∑i​L(yi​,F(xi​)), and a gradient descent step on those nnn numbers would move each one by η rmi\eta\, r_{mi}ηrmi​. That step only changes the predictions at the training points. Boosting replaces r\mathbf{r}r by the stump that fits it best, hmh_mhm​, and steps along that: Fm=Fm−1+η hmF_m = F_{m-1} + \eta\, h_mFm​=Fm−1​+ηhm​.

    Gradient boosting does classification this way, using the log-loss from logistic regression. For another loss, rmir_{mi}rmi​ is replaced by −∂L/∂F-\partial L / \partial F−∂L/∂F at Fm−1(xi)F_{m-1}(x_i)Fm−1​(xi​), and the rest of the recipe is unchanged.

Quick check

What does each new stump try to fit?

Only the first stump is fitted to y (after taking off the mean). Fitting y again would give the same stump every round.

Right. Each stump is fitted to rᵢ = yᵢ − F(xᵢ), what the model so far still gets wrong. For squared error, that is also the negative gradient of the loss.

Every training point is used in every round. Points the model already fits well have small residuals, so they pull the next stump less.

The test points are never used for fitting. They are kept aside to check the model on points it has not seen.

Why might you stop early?

With least-squares stumps and 0 < η < 2, the training error never rises. That is exactly why it cannot tell you when to stop.

Right. With η = 0.1, the test MSE (on held-out data) is lowest at M = 111 (0.097) and is 0.110 by M = 300, while the training MSE keeps falling. The later stumps fit noise.

There is always a split to make: 40 points have 39 gaps between them, and the residuals change every round. The rounds never run out.

η and M are separate settings. Stopping early changes M, not η. A smaller η usually needs more rounds, not fewer.

Lesson complete

  • Boosting builds a model as a sum: start from the mean, then add η times a small tree, round after round.
  • Each tree is fitted to the residuals of the model so far. For squared error, the residuals are the negative gradient of the loss.
  • With least-squares stumps and 0 < η < 2 the training error never rises, but the test error has a lowest point, so the number of rounds M is chosen on held-out data.
  • A smaller η takes smaller steps and needs more rounds.

Common misconceptions

  • "More rounds is always better." The training error keeps falling, but the test error turns back up.
  • "Boosting averages independent trees, like a random forest." Its trees are built one after another, and each depends on the ones before it.
  • "A smaller η is always better." It needs many more rounds for about the same result: here the best M is 207 at η = 0.05 against 111 at η = 0.1, with the same lowest test MSE, 0.097, to three decimals.

Further reading

  1. James, Witten, Hastie & Tibshirani, An Introduction to Statistical Learning (2nd ed.), Section 8.2.3, "Boosting", which boosts small regression trees by fitting each one to the current residuals. It calls η the shrinkage parameter and writes it λ, writes the number of trees as B, and notes that boosting can overfit if B is too large.
  2. Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning (2nd ed.), Section 10.10, "Numerical Optimization via Gradient Boosting", which presents boosting as steepest descent with each step approximated by a tree, and Section 10.12.1, "Shrinkage".

About the data. The points are made up. For each point, x is drawn uniformly between −1 and 1, then y = sin(πx) + 0.25 z, where z is a draw from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1, made with mulberry32. The 40 training points use seed 91 and the 300 test points seed 92. The mean and every MSE divide by the number of points. Stumps split midway between neighbouring x values. The page runs 300 rounds once for each η and keeps every round. Displayed values are rounded; the calculations are not.
