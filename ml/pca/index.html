Principal component analysis

Find the direction in which the data vary most, and keep most of the information with fewer numbers.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

k-means looked for groups without any labels. Principal component analysis, PCA for short, also works without labels, but it looks for directions. It asks: along which direction do the points spread out the most?

We centre the data, turn a line through it, find the best direction, add the second one, squeeze each person down to one number, and finish with what goes wrong when the units change.

The people are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • how to compute the direction of greatest spread instead of searching for it
  • how much of the spread one number per person keeps, and what is lost
  • why PCA depends on the units, and how standardising fixes that

  1. Centre the data

    Principal component analysis (PCA) looks at how points spread around their centre, so first move the centre to zero.

    Look at the plot: 50 grey dots, one per person, height across and arm span up, in cm. Taller people tend to have longer arms, so the cloud leans.

    Centring subtracts the means, 169.7 cm of height and 169.5 cm of arm span, from everyone, so the centre sits at 0, where the dashed lines cross. Press "Raw" or "Centred": only zero moves.

    The ringed person, (170.4, 161.8) cm, becomes (0.7, −7.6): a little taller than average, with arms well short of average.

    The maths

    Write person iii as xi=(xi1,xi2)\mathbf{x}_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2})xi​=(xi1​,xi2​) and the mean as xˉ\bar{\mathbf{x}}xˉ. The centred point is

    x~i=xi−xˉ,xˉ=1n∑i=1nxi\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{x}_ix~i​=xi​−xˉ,xˉ=n1​i=1∑n​xi​

    Stack the centred points as the rows of an n×2n \times 2n×2 matrix X~\tilde{X}X~. Each of its columns adds up to 0.

  2. Turn a line

    Now drop each person onto a line through the centre, at a right angle.

    Look at the white line, turned by the "angle" slider. Each cyan dot is a person's projection, where the person lands on the line; a grey segment joins the two.

    Along is the cyan dots' spread, their sample variance (Lesson 3); across is the same for the segments. Move the slider to the left end, 0°: along is then height's variance, 40.0 cm², and across is arm span's, 45.8 cm², 85.8 cm² in all.

    The chart plots along (cyan), across (dashed violet) and their total (dotted) for every angle. A dashed upright line marks your angle. The total never changes: what along gains, across loses.

    The maths

    To get along, square each cyan dot's distance from the centre, add them up and divide by n − 1. Across does the same with the lengths of the grey segments, which meet the line at right angles.

    A line through the centre at angle θ\thetaθ has the unit direction v=(cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)\mathbf{v} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)v=(cosθ,sinθ). Person iii lands at position ti=x~i⋅vt_i = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}ti​=x~i​⋅v along it, and sits a distance did_idi​ from it. By Pythagoras, ∥x~i∥2=ti2+di2\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i\rVert^2 = t_i^2 + d_i^2∥x~i​∥2=ti2​+di2​. Add up over everyone and divide by n−1n - 1n−1:

    1n−1∑iti2⏟along+1n−1∑idi2⏟across=s12+s22\underbrace{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i t_i^2}_{\text{along}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i d_i^2}_{\text{across}} = s_1^2 + s_2^2alongn−11​i∑​ti2​​​+acrossn−11​i∑​di2​​​=s12​+s22​

    where s12s_1^2s12​ and s22s_2^2s22​ are the sample variances of height and arm span. The right side does not depend on θ\thetaθ.

    Both parts here divide by n−1n - 1n−1. The mean squared distance to the line divides by nnn instead, so with it the identity needs a factor: along+nn−1⋅1n∑idi2=s12+s22\text{along} + \frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_i d_i^2 = s_1^2 + s_2^2along+n−1n​⋅n1​∑i​di2​=s12​+s22​.

  3. Best direction

    Along peaks in one direction: the first principal component, PC1.

    Look at the line: it sits on PC1, at 47.7°, where along is largest: 73.9 cm², written γ₁ (gamma one). "Snap to PC1" brings it back.

    PC1 can be computed from a 2 × 2 table, the covariance matrix S: the two variances and their covariance, 30.8 cm² (how height and arm span rise together). An eigenvector of S is a direction that S only stretches, never turns. The stretch is its eigenvalue. PC1 is the one with the larger eigenvalue, γ₁.

    Note: PC1 is stored as two numbers, a step in height and a step in arm span. Software may flip both signs; the line stays the same, so the signs alone mean nothing.

    The maths

    The covariance matrix, dividing by n−1n - 1n−1 (the sample covariance), is

    S=1n−1X~⊤X~=(s12s12s12s22)S = \frac{1}{n-1}\tilde{X}^{\top}\tilde{X} = \begin{pmatrix} s_1^2 & s_{12} \\ s_{12} & s_2^2 \end{pmatrix}S=n−11​X~⊤X~=(s12​s12​​s12​s22​​)

    We write it SSS, not Σ\SigmaΣ, because Σ\SigmaΣ is the summation sign on this site. The variance along a unit direction v\mathbf{v}v is

    1n−1∑i(x~i⋅v)2=v⊤S v\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 = \mathbf{v}^{\top} S\,\mathbf{v}n−11​i∑​(x~i​⋅v)2=v⊤Sv

    Maximise it subject to ∥v∥=1\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = 1∥v∥=1. Setting the gradient of v⊤Sv−γ(v⊤v−1)\mathbf{v}^{\top}S\mathbf{v} - \gamma(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{v} - 1)v⊤Sv−γ(v⊤v−1) to zero gives

    Sv=γ v,v⊤S v=γS\mathbf{v} = \gamma\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v}^{\top}S\,\mathbf{v} = \gammaSv=γv,v⊤Sv=γ

    So the best direction is an eigenvector, and the variance along it is its eigenvalue: the largest one, γ1\gamma_1γ1​. For a 2 × 2 matrix the two eigenvalues, γ1≥γ2\gamma_1 \ge \gamma_2γ1​≥γ2​, are

    γ1,2=s12+s222±(s12−s222)2+s122\gamma_{1,2} = \frac{s_1^2 + s_2^2}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{s_1^2 - s_2^2}{2}\right)^2 + s_{12}^2}γ1,2​=2s12​+s22​​±(2s12​−s22​​)2+s122​​

    Most books write eigenvalues as λ\lambdaλ. This explorer keeps λ\lambdaλ for the regularisation strength, so here they are γ\gammaγ.

    S holds height's variance, 40.0 cm², and arm span's, 45.8 cm², on its diagonal, and the covariance, 30.8 cm², in both other places. The slider's best whole degree is 48°; the exact best angle is 47.7°. PC1's direction is v₁ = (0.67, 0.74): going 1 cm along PC1 means going 0.67 cm in height and 0.74 cm in arm span. This page always makes the larger of a direction's two numbers (in size) positive.

    The loss surface lesson met eigenvalues too, as the curvatures of its bowl.

  4. PC2 and shares

    The second principal component, PC2, is at right angles to PC1.

    Look at the arrows: PC1 solid cyan, PC2 dashed violet, each two standard deviations long.

    PC2 is the other eigenvector of S. Along it the variance is γ₂ = 11.9 cm², the least of any direction.

    73.9 + 11.9 = 85.8 cm², the total. PC1 explains 86.1% of it, its share of the variance, and PC2 explains 13.9%. The cyan and violet bars show these shares.

    Note: PCA never sees an outcome (something you want to predict), so a large share need not help predict one.

    The maths

    The sum of the eigenvalues of a matrix equals the sum of its diagonal, the trace:

    γ1+γ2=tr⁡S=s12+s22,ek=γkγ1+γ2\gamma_1 + \gamma_2 = \operatorname{tr} S = s_1^2 + s_2^2, \qquad e_k = \frac{\gamma_k}{\gamma_1 + \gamma_2}γ1​+γ2​=trS=s12​+s22​,ek​=γ1​+γ2​γk​​

    Here eke_kek​ is the share of the variance explained by PCkkk; eee is a share, not a residual. Because SSS is symmetric, its eigenvectors are at right angles. Measured along v1\mathbf{v}_1v1​ and v2\mathbf{v}_2v2​, the two new coordinates have variances γ1\gamma_1γ1​ and γ2\gamma_2γ2​ and covariance 0.

    With many features, software finds the same directions with the singular value decomposition (SVD) of X~\tilde{X}X~, without forming SSS first. We do not need it here.

    Each arrow is twice the square root of its variance long: two standard deviations along PC1 and along PC2. γ₂ is also step 2's across value with the line on PC1. PC2's direction is v₂ = (0.74, −0.67): going 1 cm along PC2 means going 0.74 cm in height and −0.67 cm in arm span.

  5. One score each

    Next comes dimension reduction: one number per person, their place on PC1.

    Look at the strip: each dot is one person's score. "Rebuilt" puts everyone on the line at their score, "Original" puts them back, and grey segments show what is lost.

    The ringed person scores −5.2 and is rebuilt 5.7 cm away. The segments' mean squared length, the reconstruction error, is 11.7 cm². PC1 has the most along, so the least across (step 2): no line through the centre loses less. Keeping only the score keeps PC1's share, 86.1% of the variance.

    Note: PCA makes the right-angle grey gaps small. The regression line of arm span on height makes the up-and-down gaps small instead, so the two lines differ.

    The maths

    The score and the rebuilt point are

    ti=x~i⋅v1,x^i=xˉ+ti v1t_i = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_1, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + t_i\,\mathbf{v}_1ti​=x~i​⋅v1​,x^i​=xˉ+ti​v1​

    The lost part, xi−x^i\mathbf{x}_i - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_ixi​−x^i​, runs at right angles to the PC1 line; its length is the point's distance from the line, which is the size of its PC2 coordinate. So, with the nnn versus n−1n - 1n−1 factor written out,

    1n∑i∥xi−x^i∥2=n−1n γ2\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \lVert\mathbf{x}_i - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\rVert^2 = \frac{n-1}{n}\,\gamma_2n1​i∑​∥xi​−x^i​∥2=nn−1​γ2​

    By the identity in step 2, the line with the most variance along it has the least across it. So PC1 is also the line through the centre closest to the points, in mean squared distance. With more features, keeping the first few scores works the same way.

    To rebuild a person, start at the mean and go their score along v₁. The ringed person is rebuilt at (166.2, 165.6) cm. Their unusually short arms are lost: one number can only say where someone sits along the main trend.

    One number per person keeps 86.1% of the variance. In numbers, the reconstruction error divides by n = 50, while γ₂ = 11.9 cm², step 2's across value with the line on PC1, divides by n − 1 = 49. So the error is (49 ÷ 50) × γ₂.

  6. Units matter

    Last, PCA sees only numbers, not units.

    Look at the PC1 arrow, then press "Arm span mm". Arm-span numbers grow 10-fold and their variance 100-fold, so PC1 swings to 86.1°, 3.9° from the arm-span axis.

    Press "Standardised": each feature has its mean subtracted and is divided by its standard deviation, which cancels the factor of 10. PC1 then sits at 45.0° in cm or mm. Plain PCA does not rescale.

    Note: if the features share a unit whose spread matters, plain PCA can be right. If units differ or are arbitrary, standardise.

    The next lesson, the artificial neuron, starts Level 4, Deep learning, with a weighted sum, a bias and a squashing curve.

    The maths

    Standardising uses zij=(xij−xˉj)/σjz_{ij} = (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)/\sigma_jzij​=(xij​−xˉj​)/σj​, with the population SD σj\sigma_jσj​ (divide by nnn). The sample covariance of the z-scores is then

    Sz=nn−1 R,R=(1rr1)S_z = \frac{n}{n-1}\,R, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & r \\ r & 1 \end{pmatrix}Sz​=n−1n​R,R=(1r​r1​)

    where RRR is the correlation matrix. Multiplying a matrix by a number does not change its eigenvectors or the shares, so the choice of nnn or n−1n - 1n−1 here does not change the answer. The eigenvalues of RRR are 1±r1 \pm r1±r, with eigenvectors along (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1). That is why PC1 sits at 45° and its share is (1+r)/2(1 + r)/2(1+r)/2 (for r>0r > 0r>0; if r<0r < 0r<0, PC1 is along (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1), at 135°, with share (1−r)/2(1 - r)/2(1−r)/2; if r=0r = 0r=0, every direction ties).

    Multiplying arm span by 10 multiplies s22s_2^2s22​ by 100 and s12s_{12}s12​ by 10, but leaves rrr unchanged. So plain PCA moves and standardised PCA does not.

    In mm, arm span's variance is 4576 mm², against height's 40.0 cm². Both axes use the same number scale, so the cloud is a thin upright streak. Standardising here uses the population SD (dividing by n).

    With arm span in mm, PC1's share is 99.6%; standardised, it is 86.0%. Here r = 0.72. With more than two standardised features, the direction of PC1 is not fixed in advance.

Quick check

What does PC1 maximise?

Right. Of all directions through the centre, PC1 has the most spread along it, and that spread is the eigenvalue γ₁.

The other way round. Along and across add up to a fixed total, so the direction with the most variance along it has the least distance across it. PC1 minimises the mean squared distance.

PCA does not change the data, so the correlation stays what it is. PCA only chooses a direction to look along.

Hardly any point lies exactly on any line. PCA works with squared distances and variances, not with counts.

After switching arm span to mm, PC1 points where, and why?

Right. PC1 sits at 86.1°, only 3.9° from the arm-span axis. Multiplying arm span by 10 multiplies its variance by 100, and PC1 follows the variance.

The people are the same, but PCA sees only numbers. Bigger numbers mean a bigger variance, and PC1 follows the variance.

PC1 follows the larger variance, not the smaller one, and precision plays no part. Height now has by far the smaller variance.

Plain PCA does not rescale anything. PC1 lands at 45° only after you standardise both features yourself.

Lesson complete

  • PCA centres the data, then finds the direction with the most variance along it: PC1.
  • PC1 is the eigenvector of the covariance matrix S with the largest eigenvalue γ₁, and γ₁ is the variance along it.
  • The shares γₖ ÷ (γ₁ + γ₂) say how much of the total variance each direction carries; they add up to 1.
  • Keeping only the PC1 score loses the least, in mean squared distance, of any single line through the centre.
  • PCA depends on the units. Standardise when the features are measured in different or arbitrary units.

Common misconceptions

  • "PC1 is the regression line of arm span on height." Regression makes the up-and-down distances small and treats the two features differently. PCA makes the right-angle distances small and treats both features alike, so the two lines differ.
  • "A large share means PC1 is good for predicting an outcome." PCA never sees an outcome. The direction with the most spread need not be the one that matters for a prediction.
  • "The signs of PC1 mean something." A direction and its opposite give the same line. Software may flip the sign; the line stays put.
