Principal component analysis

Find the direction in which the data vary most, and keep most of the information with fewer numbers.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

k-means looked for groups without any labels. Principal component analysis, PCA for short, also works without labels, but it looks for directions. It asks: along which direction do the points spread out the most?

We centre the data, turn a line through it, find the best direction, add the second one, squeeze each person down to one number, and finish with what goes wrong when the units change.

The people are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • why the direction of greatest spread is the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix
  • how much of the spread one number per person keeps, and what is lost
  • why PCA depends on the units, and how standardising fixes that

  2. Spin a line

    Turn a line through the centre and drop each point onto it at a right angle. The spread along the line and the spread across it trade off.

    The small cyan dots are where each person lands on the line. The grey segments join each person to that spot, at right angles to the line. Use the angle slider to turn it.

    Along: square each cyan dot's distance from the centre, add them up and divide by n − 1. That is the sample variance of the positions along the line (Lesson 3 explains n − 1). Across: do the same with the lengths of the grey segments.

    At 0° the line is the height axis. Along is then the variance of height, cm², and across is the variance of arm span, cm². Together they make cm².

    That total, cm², is the same at every angle. Whatever spread the line gains along it, it loses across it. The chart shows the two curves, with the total as a dotted line.

  3. The best direction

    The direction with the most spread along it is the first principal component, PC1. We do not have to search for it: it can be computed.

    On the slider, the best whole degree is °. The exact best direction is °, and Snap to PC1 turns the line there. The variance along it is cm², more than at any other angle. We write it γ₁ (gamma one).

    Going 1 cm along PC1 means going cm in height and cm in arm span. That pair is the direction v₁.

    Where does it come from? Put the two variances and the covariance, all dividing by n − 1, into a 2 × 2 table called the covariance matrix, S. Height's variance is , arm span's is , and their covariance is , all in cm².

    PC1 is the eigenvector of S with the larger eigenvalue. An eigenvector is a direction that S only stretches, without turning it: Sv = γv. The stretch, γ, is its eigenvalue. The loss surface lesson met eigenvalues too, as the curvatures of its bowl.

    A direction has no arrowhead: −v₁ gives the same line. Software may hand you either sign. This page always makes the larger of its two numbers (in size) positive.

  4. PC2, and the share of the variance

    The second principal component, PC2, is at right angles to PC1. Together they split the total spread into two parts.

  5. Keep one number per person

    Keep only each person's position along PC1: one number instead of two. Then rebuild both measurements from that number.

    A person's score is their position along PC1, measured from the centre. The panel shows all 50 scores on one line.

    To rebuild a person, start at the mean and go their score along v₁. Rebuilt moves every point onto the PC1 line. The grey segments are what is lost.

    Over all 50 people, the mean squared length of the grey segments is cm². This is a mean, so it divides by n = 50, and it equals (49 ÷ 50) × γ₂.   6. Units matter

    Write arm span in millimetres and PCA gives a different answer. Standardising first takes the units out.

    These are the same 50 people, with arm span now in mm. Every arm-span number is 10 times larger, so its variance is 100 times larger: mm² against height's cm². PCA sees only the numbers, not the units.

    Arm span now carries almost all of the variance. PC1 swings to °, only ° from the arm-span axis, with a share of . Both axes use the same number scale, so the cloud is a thin upright streak.

    The fix is to standardise each feature first: subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation, here the population SD (dividing by n). Then cm or mm makes no difference: PC1 is at ° either way, with a share of .

    With exactly two standardised features, the answer is fixed by the correlation r, here . When they rise together (r > 0), PC1 lies at 45° and its share is (1 + r) ÷ 2. With more features the direction is not fixed in advance.

    So should you always standardise? If the features share a unit and their spreads matter, like two lengths in cm, plain PCA can be the right choice. If the units differ or are arbitrary, standardise.

Quick check

What does PC1 maximise?

Right. Of all directions through the centre, PC1 has the most spread along it, and that spread is the eigenvalue γ₁.

The other way round. Along and across add up to a fixed total, so the direction with the most variance along it has the least distance across it. PC1 minimises the mean squared distance.

PCA does not change the data, so the correlation stays what it is. PCA only chooses a direction to look along.

Hardly any point lies exactly on any line. PCA works with squared distances and variances, not with counts.

After switching arm span to mm, PC1 points where, and why?

Right. PC1 sits at °, only ° from the arm-span axis. Multiplying arm span by 10 multiplies its variance by 100, and PC1 follows the variance.

The people are the same, but PCA sees only numbers. Bigger numbers mean a bigger variance, and PC1 follows the variance.

PC1 follows the larger variance, not the smaller one, and precision plays no part. Height now has by far the smaller variance.

Plain PCA does not rescale anything. PC1 lands at 45° only after you standardise both features yourself.

Lesson complete

  • PCA centres the data, then finds the direction with the most variance along it: PC1.
  • PC1 is the eigenvector of the covariance matrix S with the largest eigenvalue γ₁, and γ₁ is the variance along it.
  • The shares γₖ ÷ (γ₁ + γ₂) say how much of the total variance each direction carries; they add up to 1.
  • Keeping only the PC1 score loses the least, in mean squared distance, of any single line through the centre.
  • PCA depends on the units. Standardise when the features are measured in different or arbitrary units.

Common misconceptions

  • "PC1 is the regression line of arm span on height." Regression makes the up-and-down distances small and treats the two features differently. PCA makes the right-angle distances small and treats both features alike, so the two lines differ.
  • "A large share means PC1 is good for predicting an outcome." PCA never sees an outcome. The direction with the most spread need not be the one that matters for a prediction.
  • "The signs of PC1 mean something." A direction and its opposite give the same line. Software may flip the sign; the line stays put.
