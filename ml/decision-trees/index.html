Decision trees

Label a case by asking it a few yes/no questions, each one chosen to sort the classes apart as well as it can.

About 10 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

Logistic regression and a linear support vector machine draw one straight boundary across the plot. A decision tree works differently. It asks a question about one feature, splits the cases into two groups, and then asks a new question inside each group.

We start with one question, measure how well it sorts the classes, let the tree choose its own questions, and then grow it too far on purpose.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • how a tree labels a case, and how the Gini impurity scores a split
  • why the tree chooses its questions one at a time, and what that costs
  • why a very deep tree is perfect on the training data and worse on new cases

  1. One question

    A decision tree labels a case by asking yes/no questions. Start with one.

    Look at the plot: 80 training cases, 43 cyan circles (class 0) and 37 violet triangles (class 1), placed by two features, x₁ across and x₂ up.

    The white line asks "is x₁ less than 5?". A question like this is a split. Each side predicts its majority class: yes gives class 0, no gives class 1, as the shading shows. That gets 70.0% of the training cases right.

    On the right is the same rule as a tree. The question at the top is the root; yes goes left and no goes right. Each end box is a leaf, showing its class and counts, circles · triangles.

    The maths

    Case iii has features xi=(xi1,xi2)\mathbf{x}_i = (x_{i1}, x_{i2})xi​=(xi1​,xi2​) and a label yi∈{0,1}y_i \in \{0, 1\}yi​∈{0,1}. A split on feature jjj at threshold ttt sends a case to the left if xij<tx_{ij} < txij​<t and to the right otherwise.

    A leaf predicts the class that most of its training cases have. If a leaf holds equal numbers of both, this page predicts class 0. That tie rule is a choice; another program might pick differently.

  2. Scoring a split

    To compare splits, we need a score for how mixed each side is.

    Look at the read-out: each side's circles (○) and triangles (△). The Gini impurity, G, scores how mixed one side is: 0 for one class only, 0.5 for a half-and-half mix.

    At the starting question, x₁ < 5, the yes side has G = 0.368 and the no side 0.454. Weight each by its share of the cases and add: this weighted Gini, 0.414, scores the split (the chart's "weighted G"). Lower is better.

    Slide "Threshold": the chart plots the weighted Gini at every threshold on x₁. It moves in steps, changing only when the line passes a point. Its lowest step includes 5.

    The maths

    With pkp_kpk​ the share of class kkk in a group of cases (here ppp is a share of cases, not a predicted probability),

    G=1−∑kpk2G = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2G=1−k∑​pk2​

    With two classes, p1=pp_1 = pp1​=p and p0=1−pp_0 = 1 - pp0​=1−p, so G=1−(1−p)2−p2=2p(1−p)G = 1 - (1-p)^2 - p^2 = 2p(1-p)G=1−(1−p)2−p2=2p(1−p).

    If a split sends nLn_LnL​ of the nnn cases left and nRn_RnR​ right, its weighted Gini is

    Gsplit=nLn GL+nRn GRG_{\text{split}} = \frac{n_L}{n}\,G_L + \frac{n_R}{n}\,G_RGsplit​=nnL​​GL​+nnR​​GR​

    The thresholds a tree tries are the midpoints between neighbouring values of the feature, once sorted. Any threshold between the same two values makes the same split, so nothing is missed.

    Why not score a split by how many cases it gets right? A split can leave the majority unchanged on both sides, so the count of errors stays the same, yet make one side much purer. Gini notices that; a count of errors would not.

    Before any split, all 80 cases together have G = 0.497.

    The lowest step runs from 4.918 to 5.001, just above 5, with weighted Gini 0.414. Every threshold in it makes the same split. A tree uses its midpoint, 4.96, so our guess of 5 happened to land in it.

  3. Best first split

    The tree tries every threshold, as you did with the slider, on both features, and keeps the lowest weighted Gini.

    Look at the chart: x₁'s curve is white and solid, x₂'s is cyan and dashed. x₁'s curve is lowest at threshold 4.96 (0.414), and x₂'s at 3.04 (0.400), as the read-out lists.

    So the x₂ split wins, and the tree's first question is "is x₂ less than 3.04?": the solid line on the plot. The dashed line is x₁'s best.

    This choice is greedy: the best split right now, with no looking ahead. Then each side becomes a new node, a box in the tree, and gets its own best question, and so on down. This is called recursive binary splitting.

    The maths

    At each node the tree picks the feature jjj and midpoint ttt with the lowest GsplitG_{\text{split}}Gsplit​. On this page a tie goes to the lower feature number, then to the lower threshold.

    A node becomes a leaf when it is pure (G=0G = 0G=0), when no split lowers its impurity, or when it reaches the maximum depth.

  4. Growing the tree

    Repeat that search inside each side, and the tree grows.

    Move the "Max depth" slider from 1 to 8 and watch the shading. Max depth caps how many questions in a row a case is asked.

    Each question compares one feature with a number, so every cut runs straight across or straight up: the regions are boxes with sides parallel to the axes. The white line marks where the predicted class changes.

    Rescaling a feature only moves its thresholds, so trees need no standardising.

    From depth 6 all 18 leaves are pure (one class each), and growth stops. The diagram draws three levels below the root; dashed "+n" boxes hide n deeper splits.

    The maths

    The depth of a leaf is the number of questions on the way to it. A tree of depth ddd has at most 2d2^d2d leaves, and fewer when some branches stop early.

    Max depth is one way to stop a tree. Others set a smallest leaf size, or grow a big tree and then cut back the branches that help least, which is called pruning.

    At max depth 2 one leaf holds 1 circle and 1 triangle. That is a tie, and this page sends ties to class 0, as step 1's maths says.

  5. Too deep

    A deeper tree fits its training cases better. What about new cases?

    Look at the chart: accuracy by depth on the 80 training cases (white line) and 400 test cases (cyan dashes), never used to grow the tree. Move "Max depth" to read both.

    From depth 6 every leaf is pure, so each training case lands in a leaf of its own class: 100% training accuracy, even for the 8 labels flipped at random. The tree has learned the noise. Test accuracy peaks at depth 2 (ringed), at 82.5%, then falls to 78.3% for the full tree. That gap between training (100%) and test is overfitting.

    Note: in practice you pick the depth on a separate validation set, keeping the test set for the final check.

    The maths

    Each label was flipped with probability 0.1, wherever the point lies. So even the rule that made the data is expected to be wrong on 10% of new cases, and no classifier can expect to do better than 90% on new cases here.

    Here no two training cases share a value of x₁ or of x₂, so some split always lowers the impurity until every leaf is pure. Two cases with the same features and different labels could never be separated, and repeated values can stall a tree earlier. On this data, then, 100% training accuracy comes from how the tree grows, not from anything it has learned about new cases.

    For scale, the rule that made the data scores 90.0% on the test cases, because 40 of their 400 labels were flipped too.

  6. Reading a tree

    Now label a new case with step 5's best tree, depth 2.

    Look for the hollow diamond, a new case. Drag it, or use Tab and arrow keys. At (2, 8): is x₂ less than 3.04? No, so go right. Is x₁ less than 4.93? Yes, so go left. Its leaf holds 19 circles and 8 triangles, so it predicts class 0. The dashed box is its region.

    Greedy choices are not always the best overall. A depth-2 tree starting with x₁ < 4.96, turned down in step 3, has purer leaves: its total leaf impurity (the leaves' weighted Gini) is 0.221, against the greedy tree's 0.266. Greedy never looks two questions ahead.

    The next lesson, Bagging and random forests, lets many trees vote.

    The maths

    The total leaf impurity of a tree is ∑leavesnℓn Gℓ\sum_{\text{leaves}} \frac{n_\ell}{n}\,G_\ell∑leaves​nnℓ​​Gℓ​, where leaf ℓ\ellℓ holds nℓn_\ellnℓ​ of the nnn training cases. The comparison above tried every first question on this data, each followed by the best split on each side.

    Searching every possible tree is hopeless beyond tiny examples: the number of trees grows far too fast. So trees are grown greedily, and the result is a good tree rather than the best possible one.

    Lower impurity on the training cases is not the same as higher accuracy on new ones. The point here is only that the greedy tree is not the best tree by its own measure.

    The rule that made the data says class 1 at (2, 8): it sits in the small corner where x₁ < 3 and x₂ > 7. The depth-2 tree is too shallow to carve that corner out. The depth-3 tree does, yet scores lower on the test cases overall (81.3% against 82.5%).

Quick check

Why does a fully grown tree get 100% training accuracy?

No. The rule itself is wrong on the cases whose labels were flipped. The full tree gets those right too, which means it has learned the noise, and on the test cases it scores 78.3% against the rule's 90.0%.

Right. On this data the tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, so each training case is predicted correctly by construction. That says nothing about new cases.

The tree never sees the test cases while it grows. Its leaves are pure only for the training cases; on the test cases the full tree scores 78.3%.

The data are noisy on purpose: 8 of the 80 training labels were flipped. The full tree still gets 100%, because it memorises those cases too.

At the root, which split does the tree choose?

That is the best split on x₁, but its weighted Gini is higher. The tree keeps the split with the lowest weighted Gini.

Right. Its weighted Gini, 0.400, is the lower of the two, so the tree asks about x₂ first.

Equal sizes are not the aim; pure sides are. The weighted Gini already takes each side's size into account, and the x₂ split scores lower.

The tree never looks at the test cases. It chooses splits by their weighted Gini on the training cases.

Lesson complete

  • A decision tree labels a case by a chain of yes/no questions, each about one feature. Its regions are boxes with sides parallel to the axes.
  • Each split is chosen to give the lowest weighted Gini impurity, G = 1 − Σ pₖ², which is 2p(1 − p) with two classes.
  • The search is greedy: the best split now, with no looking ahead. That keeps it fast, but the tree it finds is not always the best one.
  • Depth is the overfitting knob. A full tree is perfect on the training data and learns its noise; choose the depth on a validation set.

Common misconceptions

  • "The tree finds the best possible set of questions." It finds a good set, one question at a time. A different first question can lead to a better tree.
  • "A deeper tree is a better tree." Past a point it fits the noise in the training data, and new cases suffer.
  • "Trees need their features standardised, like k-NN." Multiplying a feature by a positive number moves its thresholds with it, and every split separates the same cases.

Further reading

  1. James, Witten, Hastie & Tibshirani, An Introduction to Statistical Learning (2nd ed.), Section 8.1, "The Basics of Decision Trees", which grows trees by recursive binary splitting, described there as top-down and greedy.
  2. The same book, Section 8.1.2, "Classification Trees", which defines the Gini index. It writes it as a sum of p(1 − p) over the classes, which equals 1 − Σ pₖ².

About the data. The cases are made up. Each point has x₁ = 10u₁ and x₂ = 10u₂, where u₁ and u₂ are uniform draws between 0 and 1 from mulberry32. Its class is 1 if (x₁ > 5 and x₂ > 3) or (x₁ < 3 and x₂ > 7), and 0 otherwise. Then a third draw u₃ flips the label if u₃ < 0.1. The draws come in the order u₁, u₂, u₃ for one point, then the next. The 80 training cases use seed 99 and the 400 test cases seed 100; 8 training labels and 40 test labels were flipped. The new case starts at (2, 8), chosen inside the small corner of the rule. Thresholds sit midway between neighbouring values; ties between splits go to the lower feature, then the lower threshold; a leaf tie goes to class 0. Displayed values are rounded; the calculations are not.
