Measuring error

Every model misses. A loss sums up all the misses in one number.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

A model's predictions miss. To say how good a model is, or to train one, we need a single number that sums up how badly it misses. That number is called a loss. Smaller is better.

This lesson takes 11 points and one fixed line, and builds two losses from the line's misses: the mean absolute error (MAE) and the mean squared error (MSE). Lesson 1 promised to explain why we square. This is where it does.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • what a residual is, and why residuals can't simply be added up
  • how MAE and MSE are built, and how each one treats a single big miss
  • why squaring is the usual choice, and which single guess each loss prefers

  2. Why not just add them up?

    Adding the residuals sounds like a fair total. But misses above the line are positive and misses below are negative, so they cancel.

    The chart on the right lays the sticks end to end. The ones above the line go up (solid). Then the ones below come back down (dashed).

    The walk ends where it started: the residuals add up to zero, yet every point misses.

    That is no accident. For a least-squares line like this one, the residuals always add up to zero. So the plain sum tells you nothing about how well the line fits.

    We need a total in which every miss counts against the line, whatever its sign. There are two common fixes: use the size of each miss, or its square.

  4. MSE: square each miss

    Or square each miss and average the squares. That is the mean squared error, or MSE: the "average squared miss" from Lesson 1.

    Squaring a miss gives the area of a square with that side. Each stick now carries its square.

  6. The best constant guess

    Suppose you had to guess one number, c, for every point, ignoring x. Which c is best? It depends on the loss: the mean of y for MSE, the median of y for MAE.

    The line is now flat at height c, and each stick is a point's miss from c. Move the slider and watch the two curves on the right. Each curve gives the loss for every possible c, and the dots mark the current one.

    The MSE is lowest when c is the mean of y, . The MAE is lowest when c is the median of y, . These are the mean and median from Lesson 3.

    There are 11 points, an odd number, so the median is a single middle value and the lowest MAE is at that one c. With an even number of points, every c between the two middle values would give the same lowest MAE.

    The chart shows the RMSE, the square root of the MSE, so that both curves are in the units of y. The MSE itself is in squared units. The RMSE is lowest exactly where the MSE is.

    Look at the shapes. The MSE curve is smooth. The MAE curve has a corner at each value of y.

    So why square? It stops misses cancelling, and it punishes big misses more. It is smooth, so it has a slope everywhere, which makes it easy to follow downhill with gradient descent (Lesson 7). It leads to a formula for the best line (Lesson 6). And its best constant guess is the familiar mean.

    MAE has its own strength: one far-out point sways it less, just as one far-out value moves the median less than the mean.

Quick check

Which of these punishes one big miss most heavily?

MAE counts a miss by its size. Make one miss ten times as big and its part of the MAE is ten times as big, no more.

In the plain sum, a big miss above the line can be cancelled by misses below it, so it may not count against the line at all.

Right. MSE squares each miss, so one miss ten times as big gives a square a hundred times as big.

They differ. A miss's part of the MAE grows in proportion to its size, its part of the MSE grows with the square, and in the plain sum it can be cancelled out.

You must guess one number, c, for all 11 points. Which c gives the lowest MAE?

The mean gives the lowest MSE. Here the mean and the median differ, and the MAE is lowest at the median.

Halfway between the extremes looks at only two values. It makes the biggest single miss as small as possible, not the average miss.

Zero is best only when the median itself is zero. Here every y is above zero, so c = 0 misses every point by its whole value.

Right. Moving c towards the middle brings it closer to more points than it moves away from, so the MAE falls until c reaches the middle value. With 11 points that is a single value, the median.

Lesson complete

  • A residual is a miss, y − ŷ. Positive and negative residuals cancel, so their plain sum hides how badly a line fits.
  • MAE averages the sizes of the misses. MSE averages their squares, so one big miss counts for much more.
  • For a single constant guess, MSE is lowest at the mean and MAE at the median.

Common misconceptions

  • "Residuals that add up to zero mean a perfect fit." The least-squares line always makes them add up to zero, however big the misses are.
  • "MSE is in the same units as y." It is in squared units. Its square root, the RMSE, is in the units of y.
  • "MAE and MSE always agree on the best answer." For a single constant guess, MSE picks the mean and MAE picks the median, and these often differ.
  • "Squaring is the only correct way to measure error." It is a choice, with good reasons behind it. When a few far-off points should not dominate, MAE can suit better.

About the data. The 11 points are made up. They come from a seeded random number generator, mulberry32 with seed 5. For each point in turn, x is drawn uniformly between 0 and 10, and then y = 2 + 0.8x plus a random draw from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1. So the points are the same for everyone. Here the misses come from that added noise; in real data they come from everything a model leaves out. The line is the least-squares line for these 11 points, and it stays fixed.
