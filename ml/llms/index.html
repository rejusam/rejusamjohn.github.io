Large language models, in miniature

Most large language models today are transformers trained to predict the next token on a very large amount of text. Train a tiny one here, live, watch its writing go from noise to words, and then check how many of the sentences it writes are true.

About 12 minutes · The model trains in your browser in about a minute on a laptop; every number is computed in your browser, and the formulas are in The maths

Overview

In Predicting the next token a model counted which character followed the last few, and wrote text by sampling. Here the job is the same, but the model is the causal transformer of Transformers, built on Attention: it can look back at up to 32 characters, and it learns its probabilities by training rather than counting.

The text it learns from describes the small world invented for this level: eight made-up people, the food each one likes and the place each one lives. Its facts are rules of this page, not claims about anyone. Because the facts are fixed, every sentence the model writes can be checked.

You'll learn

  • that a large language model is trained on one job, predicting the next token, and writes by sampling one token at a time
  • how a tiny one learns: its held-out loss falls and its samples turn from noise into words
  • why fluent output can be false: the model samples likely text, and nothing checks it against the facts

  1. Same job, more context

    As with the counting models, the job is to predict the next character. This model can look up to 32 characters back.

    Look at the picture: under each character is the next one, which the model must predict there. That is one prediction per position.

    In the outlined column, after "tom likes ", the food depends on the name, 10 characters back. The counting model with n = 5 sees only "ikes␣", so it gives the letter "a" only 0.26: two of the eight people like apples.

    This model is one transformer block, made causal: through attention, each position takes a weighted mix of itself and earlier characters only. So it can learn to use the name.

    The maths

    Take a window of characters x1,…,xT+1x_1, \dots, x_{T+1}x1​,…,xT+1​ with T = 32. The model reads x1,…,xTx_1, \dots, x_Tx1​,…,xT​ and gives, at each position t, a probability for every character of the vocabulary V\mathcal{V}V (here ∣V∣=28|\mathcal{V}| = 28∣V∣=28). The loss for the window is the mean cross-entropy of the true next characters:

    L=−1T∑t=1Tlog⁡p(xt+1∣x1,…,xt).\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t).L=−T1​t=1∑T​logp(xt+1​∣x1​,…,xt​).

    The causal mask is what makes this one sum honest. In the attention softmax, position t leaves out every position s > t, so its prediction depends on x1,…,xtx_1, \dots, x_tx1​,…,xt​ only and never on the answer xt+1x_{t+1}xt+1​. One forward pass therefore gives all 32 predictions at once, and one backward pass trains all of them. A batch's loss is the mean over its windows.

    The block has two attention heads, as in Transformers. Its 28 tokens are the characters of the invented world: the letters, the space and the full stop, as in Tokens: text as numbers.

  2. Train it

    Now train it. Play steps through the lesson, and Train trains the model: 2,500 steps.

    Look at the line: bits per character, the average cost of each true next character (lower is better), on 300 held-out sentences never trained on. Dashed: counting models.

    Over the full run the loss falls and ends just above the n = 5 counting line.

    Under the picture, the model writes a sample every 100 steps: noise at first, and from step 1,100 on every complete word is a word of this world.

    Note: this short run does not beat counting: on this small, regular world, counting the last 5 characters already does well.

    The maths

    Training works in natural logarithms (nats). Bits are nats divided by ln 2:

    H=−1N∑i=1Nlog⁡2pi=1Nln⁡2∑i=1N(−ln⁡pi),H = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log_2 p_i = \frac{1}{N \ln 2}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(-\ln p_i\big),H=−N1​i=1∑N​log2​pi​=Nln21​i=1∑N​(−lnpi​),

    where pip_ipi​ is the probability the model gave the true character at scored position i, and N = 6,240. The held-out text is read in windows of 32, so a scored character has between 1 and 32 characters before it in its window; the counting lines use their last n. Each sample character is drawn from the model's probabilities given the last 32 characters, as in step 3.

    Each training step takes 8 windows of 33 characters, each starting at a point drawn uniformly at random, from the training text of 2,000 sentences (42,030 characters), and makes one Adam update (from optimisers) with η = 0.003. A run takes about a minute on a laptop, and two minutes or more on a slow phone. Every 100 steps the page measures the model on the held-out text and writes a sample of 60 characters at τ = 1.

    The loss starts at 4.95 bits, about the cost of an even guess over all 28 characters (4.81). All four lines are measured on exactly the same 6,240 characters (from the 6th character on, so the n = 5 model always has its 5 characters of context). The counting lines are at 1.99, 0.47 and 0.39 bits. In the stated run the model is below the n = 1 line from step 200, below the n = 3 line from step 800 on, and first dips below the n = 5 line at step 2,400. It ends at 0.393 bits against the n = 5 line's 0.391. The previous lesson's longer contexts do better still.

    Of the complete words in each sample (the first and last may be cut off), 0% are words of the invented world at step 0, and from step 700 on at least 90% are. Each sample starts after ". " and uses its own seed, so watching the run or not gives the same samples. With reduced motion, Train computes the run without drawing it, then shows the end. Reset goes back to the same starting weights, and the next run is the same as the last.

  3. Type a prompt

    Now let it write. Type a start, a prompt, in the box under the picture.

    Look at the bars: the probability of each next character, from the model as it is now. If the read-out says "untrained", press "Train" in step 2. Try "zoe likes ", then "zoe lives ", then a name the world does not have.

    "Continue" draws one character at random, using the bar heights as odds, and adds it. Press it again and again to write on.

    The "temperature τ" slider reshapes the bars: τ = 1 is the model's own probabilities (dashed outlines), a smaller τ sharpens them, and τ = 0 always takes the likeliest (greedy).

    The maths

    At the last position the model gives a score zvz_vzv​ (a logit) for every character v, and the probabilities at temperature τ are

    qτ(v)=softmax⁡ ⁣(zτ)v=ezv/τ∑u∈Vezu/τ.q_\tau(v) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{\tau}\right)_v = \frac{e^{z_v/\tau}}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}} e^{z_u/\tau}}.qτ​(v)=softmax(τz​)v​=∑u∈V​ezu​/τezv​/τ​.

    This is the same as the previous lesson's p(v)1/τp(v)^{1/\tau}p(v)1/τ, normalised, since log⁡p(v)\log p(v)logp(v) and zvz_vzv​ differ by the same constant for every v. τ = 1 gives the model's own probabilities.

    Capitals become lower case, and the box takes up to 80 characters. The model sees the last 32 known characters, outlined under the box. A character outside the 28, such as a digit, shows as a dashed ? chip and is skipped. What you type is never stored or sent.

  4. Fluent is not the same as true

    Any food after "ana likes " makes a fluent sentence; only one is true.

    Look at the two bars, for "<name> likes " and "<name> lives ": the exact chance, averaged over the 8 people, that the sentence is true, false (a real food or place, but the wrong one) or anything else. If untrained, train it in step 2.

    After the run, at τ = 1, a false place gets 0.51, more than the true one. Greedy (τ = 0) gets the true place for only 4 of 8. Shape came before facts: at step 500, well-formed food sentences got 0.72, the true one 0.19.

    This is often called "hallucination": fluent output that is false. The model has only weights, no facts table, and nothing checks its sentences against the facts.

    The maths

    The model writes a sentence one character at a time, so the probability of a whole continuation is the product of its characters' probabilities, each given everything before it:

    p(apples.∣ana likes )=qτ(a∣ana likes )×qτ(p∣ana likes a)×⋯×qτ(.∣ana likes apples).p(\text{apples.} \mid \text{ana likes }) = q_\tau(\mathrm{a} \mid \text{ana likes }) \times q_\tau(\mathrm{p} \mid \text{ana likes a}) \times \dots \times q_\tau(. \mid \text{ana likes apples}).p(apples.∣ana likes )=qτ​(a∣ana likes )×qτ​(p∣ana likes a)×⋯×qτ​(.∣ana likes apples).

    The true probability is this product for the person's own food (or place); the false probability is the sum of the products for the other foods (or the other places, each with its own "by" or "in"). Ending with the full stop makes these different sentences, so the sums do not overlap, and anything else gets 1−ptrue−pfalse1 - p_{\text{true}} - p_{\text{false}}1−ptrue​−pfalse​. The bars show the mean of each over the 8 people.

    The bars and the numbers here are for the stated run. "False" means a real food or place of this world, but the wrong one. "Write 16 sentences" samples one sentence per prompt and marks each one ✓ true, ✗ false or – other; the counts add up press by press.

    At τ = 1, the mean probability of the true food rises from 0.19 at step 500 to 0.36 at step 1,000, 0.69 at 1,500, 0.74 at 2,000 and 0.85 at 2,500; a false food keeps 0.11. The true place rises more slowly: 0.13, 0.28, 0.34, 0.41 and 0.45. The model gives each person's own "by" or "in" 0.94 on average, but of the two places that share that word, the true one gets only 0.50, about half.

    Temperature changes the odds at the end of the run (true · false · anything else):

    • τ = 0: greedy writes one sentence per prompt, always a fact sentence: the true food for 7 of the 8 people and the true place for 4, a false one for the rest.
    • τ = 0.5: food 0.93 · 0.07 · 0.00; place 0.49 · 0.51 · 0.00.
    • τ = 1: food 0.85 · 0.11 · 0.04; place 0.45 · 0.51 · 0.04.
    • τ = 1.5: food 0.62 · 0.16 · 0.21; place 0.31 · 0.39 · 0.30.

    From τ = 0.5 up, a higher τ takes probability away from the true sentence. For foods, each step up in τ makes false sentences more likely; for places, going from τ = 1 to 1.5 sends probability to broken text instead. Longer training raises the true share, as the numbers above show, but sampling from a model never guarantees a true sentence.

  5. Scale it up

    Made very much larger, the same recipe is a large language model.

    Look at the bars: where the 11,324 weights and biases sit. The model trained on 42,030 characters and made 640,000 next-character predictions (2,500 steps × 8 windows × 32 positions).

    Real large language models use the same idea at far larger scale: pieces of words as tokens (here, single characters), many blocks (here, one), far longer windows, far more weights and text, the same loss.

    Their sentences come the same way as here: probabilities, then sampling.

    The maths

    With d = 32, two heads of width 16, a per-position network of 64 units and 28 tokens, the count part by part is: token embeddings 28×3228 \times 3228×32, position embeddings 32×3232 \times 3232×32, attention 4×32×32=40964 \times 32 \times 32 = 40964×32×32=4096 (queries, keys, values and the output map), the per-position network 64×32+64+32×64+32=419264 \times 32 + 64 + 32 \times 64 + 32 = 419264×32+64+32×64+32=4192, three layer norms with 2×322 \times 322×32 each, 192 in all, and the output layer 28×32+28=92428 \times 32 + 28 = 92428×32+28=924:

  6. After pretraining

    All of this is pretraining: predicting the next token on a lot of text. The models people talk to are usually trained further.

    Look at the boxes. Instruction tuning trains on requests paired with good replies, so the likeliest continuation of a request is a reply. Preference tuning then trains towards replies people preferred when shown two or more.

    Both change the probabilities and can make true replies likelier. Neither adds a check of each sentence against the facts. This lesson's model stops at pretraining.

    Sampling from learned probabilities is not only for text: the next lesson makes new data the same way.

    The maths

    Instruction tuning usually keeps the next-token cross-entropy of step 1, applied to the example replies. Preference tuning uses a different loss, built from pairs of replies of which people preferred one; the details vary from method to method.

Quick check

What is the model trained to do?

Right. The loss is the cross-entropy of the next character at every position, so training raises the probability of whatever came next in the training text.

There is no table inside the model, only weights. Any link between a name and its food has to come out of predicting the next character, and at the end of this run the link for places is still weak.

Nothing in the loss says true or false. It rewards giving probability to what followed in the training text; the false sentences in step 4 come from the same probabilities.

It is scored on held-out text it never saw, and its samples are drawn at random from its probabilities. A sample can repeat a training sentence, but it is not read from the text: the false sentences of step 4 never occur in it.

Why can a fluent sentence from the model be false?

Right. At the end of the run a false place gets 0.51 of the probability at τ = 1, more than the true one.

A model has no aims. It gives probabilities and a random draw picks from them; a false sentence is just a likely string of characters.

Every likes and lives sentence in the training text is true in the invented world. The false ones come from probabilities that have not linked each name to its facts.

At τ = 0 the model writes its likeliest sentence, and at the end of the run that is the true place for only 4 of the 8 people.

Lesson complete

  • The large language models people talk to are mostly causal transformers trained to predict the next token; the causal mask lets one window train every position at once.
  • It writes by sampling one token at a time from its probabilities, and temperature reshapes them.
  • Its sentences are likely, not checked: a fluent sentence can be false, and here the model learned word shapes before it linked names to facts.

Common misconceptions

  • "A lower loss means the model tells the truth." The held-out loss here ends just above the n = 5 counting line, yet a false place still gets more probability than the true one.
  • "Greedy decoding gives the correct answer." It gives the likeliest one; at the end of this run greedy writes the true place for only 4 of the 8 people.
  • "The model looks facts up." It has only weights; what it gives a fact is a probability learned from the training text.

About the data. The world is invented: eight made-up people (ana, ben, eli, mia, noa, sam, tom, zoe), six foods and four places, with a fixed table of who likes which food and lives where. Its facts are rules of this page, not claims about anyone. All the text is made in your browser by the mulberry32 random number generator: the training text is 2,000 sentences from seed 261 and the held-out text 300 sentences from seed 262, by the rule of Predicting the next token (for each sentence, one uniform draw u and a person chosen uniformly: "<person> likes <their food>." if u < 0.4, "<person> lives by/in the <their place>." if u < 0.8, otherwise "<person> and <friend> are friends."). The model's starting weights use seed 292, and the training windows are drawn with seed 291. The sample at step s uses seed 293 + s; the sentences of the k-th press of Write 16 sentences use seed 294 + k (from k = 0); Continue draws from one stream with seed 295.
