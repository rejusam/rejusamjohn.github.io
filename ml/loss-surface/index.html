Many features and the loss surface

With two numbers to learn, the loss becomes a surface. Its shape decides how easily gradient descent finds the bottom.

About 14 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

Lesson 7 learned one number. Here the model learns two, the intercept and the slope of a line, so the loss depends on both. We draw it as a map with height lines.

The feature x in this lesson runs from about 100 to 300, the size of a floor area in square metres, say. Numbers that size make the map a long, narrow valley, and gradient descent struggles in it. Standardising x fixes that.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • how to read a contour map of a loss with two inputs
  • what the gradient is, and why gradient descent steps against it
  • why an unscaled feature makes gradient descent bounce and crawl
  • how standardising makes the bowl round, without changing the fitted line
  • what the condition number measures

  1. Two numbers, one surface

    The model is the line ŷ = a + w·x. Every pair of values (a, w) has a loss, and all the pairs together make a surface. We draw it from above, as a contour map.

    a is the intercept, as in Lesson 6. w is the slope, called the weight, as in Lesson 7. This time we do not centre the data, so both numbers have to be learned.

    Each point on the map is one pair (a, w), so one line. Its loss is the mean squared error (MSE) of that line on the data. The stage works the MSE out on a fine grid of pairs and joins up the pairs with equal loss. Each closed ring is one such height line. Inner rings are lower, and the violet square marks the lowest point.

    The two axes are stretched by different amounts: one unit of w is far taller on screen than one unit of a. Without that, the rings would be too thin to see.

  2. The gradient points uphill

    The gradient puts the slope of the loss in each direction together. It points uphill, so gradient descent steps the opposite way.

    Hold w fixed and nudge a: the loss changes at some rate, the slope in the a direction. Do the same for w. These two slopes are partial derivatives, and together they are the gradient, written ∇L. Lesson 5 gave a first look at it.

    Wherever the gradient is not zero, it points the way the loss rises fastest, uphill. The dashed arrow shows it. A step of gradient descent goes the opposite way, downhill, shown by the solid arrow.

    The read-out gives the two slopes. At most positions the slope for w is far bigger than the slope for a. Nudge w by some amount and each prediction moves x times as far as when you nudge a by the same amount, and here x is between 100 and 300.

    The arrows show direction only; their length is fixed. Because the axes are stretched, they need not look square-on to the rings.

  5. Why: the condition number

    The steepest direction limits how big a step can be. The gentlest direction decides how many steps you need. Their ratio, the condition number, says how hard the bowl is.

    The curvature in a direction is how fast the slope changes as you move that way: a high curvature means a steep, tight wall. The arrows mark the steepest and gentlest directions through the bottom of the raw bowl.

    η has to suit the steepest direction, or the steps there grow without end. In the gentle direction that same η is tiny, so each step there covers only a small fraction of the distance left. That is the crawl.

    The condition number κ (kappa) is the steepest curvature divided by the gentlest. For raw x it is ( against ). For standardised x both curvatures are , so κ = , the best possible.

    Why so large for raw x? Its values are big and far from 0, so a and w get tangled: raise w a little and you must lower a a lot to keep the line near the points. Centring x untangles them, and dividing by the SD makes the two curvatures equal.

    The maths
    κ=γmax⁡γmin⁡\kappa = \frac{\gamma_{\max}}{\gamma_{\min}}κ=γmin​γmax​​

    Stability needs 0<η<2/γmax⁡0 < \eta < 2/\gamma_{\max}0<η<2/γmax​. Then along the gentle direction

    q2=1−η γmin⁡>1−2κq_2 = 1 - \eta\,\gamma_{\min} > 1 - \frac{2}{\kappa}q2​=1−ηγmin​>1−κ2​

    so each step removes less than a fraction 2/κ2/\kappa2/κ of the distance left along it. With a large κ\kappaκ and η\etaη a fixed fraction of its limit, the number of steps grows roughly in proportion to κ\kappaκ.

    Most books write these eigenvalues as λ\lambdaλ. This explorer keeps λ\lambdaλ for the regularisation strength (Lesson 10), so here they are γ\gammaγ. Some books also define the condition number from the data matrix instead of the Hessian; that version is the square root of this one.

  6. More weights, the same idea

    A model with many features has one weight per feature. You can no longer draw the surface, but everything in this lesson still applies.

    With features x₁, x₂, …, x_p the model is ŷ = a + w₁x₁ + … + w_p x_p. The loss now depends on p + 1 numbers. The gradient has one slope per number, and each step of gradient descent moves all of them at once.

    For a linear model like this with squared loss, the surface is still a bowl with one bottom, as long as no feature can be worked out exactly from the others. It has a curvature in every direction, and its condition number still sets how hard gradient descent finds it.

    So each feature is standardised with its own mean and SD before training. That fixes features measured on very different scales. It does not fix two features that carry nearly the same information: a narrow valley can remain.

    Other models, such as neural networks, have losses that are not bowls (Lesson 7, step 6). Scaling the inputs still usually helps them, but the neat picture here no longer holds exactly.

    On the right: the standardised run, mapped back, lies on top of the least-squares line.

Quick check

On raw x, why does gradient descent bounce from wall to wall across the valley?

A smaller η would bounce less, and below half the stable limit it would not bounce at all. But it would crawl along the valley even more slowly. The bounce comes from the steep walls.

Right. The steep walls dominate the gradient, so each step points mostly across the valley. With η near its limit it lands on the far wall, while the move along the valley is tiny.

This loss is a single bowl with one bottom. The bounces are back and forth across one valley.

Every step uses all the data and the same formula, so nothing jumps at random. The curvatures come from x alone, through H; the noise in y moves the bottom but not the shape of the valley.

What does standardising x change?

Mapped back to the original units, the standardised fit gives the same intercept and slope as least squares on raw x.

Right. The condition number drops to 1, and gradient descent needs far fewer steps.

Each standardised pair makes the same predictions as some raw pair, so the losses on offer are the same, and so is the lowest one.

Only x is rescaled. The values to predict stay as they are.

Lesson complete

  • With two numbers to learn, the loss is a surface. A contour map shows it from above, with rings of equal loss.
  • The gradient collects the slopes in each direction. It points uphill, and gradient descent steps against it.
  • An unscaled feature makes a long, narrow valley. η must suit the steep walls, so the run bounces across and crawls along. The condition number measures this.
  • Standardising makes the bowl round, with condition number 1, and leaves the fitted line unchanged.

Common misconceptions

  • "Standardising changes the model's answer." For this linear model, mapped back, it gives the same line. It only makes the answer easier to find.
  • "To go faster along the valley, raise the learning rate." Past 2/γmax the steps across the steep walls grow without end.
  • "Gradient descent heads straight for the bottom." It heads downhill from where it is, which in a narrow valley is mostly across it.

About the data. The 30 points are made up. They come from a seeded random number generator, mulberry32 with seed 13. For each point in turn, x is drawn uniformly between 100 and 300, and then y = 50 + 0.9x plus 15 times a random draw from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1. The points miss the line only because of that added noise; real data also has errors from measurement and from things the model leaves out. Both runs start at the line ŷ = 0 and use η = 1.8 divided by the steepest curvature of their own bowl. The step counts use the target "extra loss below one millionth of its starting value" and come from the formula in step 3; the standardised count was also checked by running the steps. The contour rings are at 25, 100, 400, 1,600, 6,400 and 25,600 above the lowest MSE. Standardising uses the population SD. The stage rounds values for display; the calculations use unrounded values.
