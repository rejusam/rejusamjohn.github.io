Overfitting and regularisation

A model can be too simple or too flexible. Both do badly on new data, for different reasons.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

We fit curves of rising flexibility to the same 15 points and score each one on 200 test points, as in Lesson 9. The training error keeps falling. The test error does not.

Then we look at why, through bias and variance, and at one fix: a penalty on large coefficients, called ridge regression.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • what underfitting and overfitting look like, in the curve and in the scores
  • what bias and variance mean, and how they add up to the test error
  • how a ridge penalty, with strength λ, calms a model that is too flexible

  6. Ridge: a penalty on big coefficients

    Keep degree 12, but add a penalty for large coefficients, the θs. Its strength is λ (lambda). The right λ calms the curve.

    Ridge regression chooses the θs to make the training MSE plus λ times (θ₁² + … + θ₁₂²) as small as possible. Wild swings need large coefficients, so the penalty pushes against them. θ₀ is not penalised, so the curve can still sit at any height.

    The slider runs from λ = 10⁻⁶ to 10 on a log scale: each notch multiplies λ by the same amount, about 1.26. The read-out also gives the largest of θ₁ to θ₁₂ in size.

    As λ grows, the training MSE rises: the penalty gives up some fit to the training points for a calmer curve. In practice λ is chosen like the degree, by validation.

Lesson complete

  • Too simple a model misses the pattern (underfitting). Too flexible a model learns the noise (overfitting). Both do badly on new data.
  • With no penalty, the training error never rises with the degree. The test error typically falls and then rises.
  • The expected test error at a point is bias squared plus variance plus the noise variance σ².
  • Ridge adds λ times the sum of the squared coefficients, leaving the intercept out. A well-chosen λ tames a flexible model.

Common misconceptions

  • "A more flexible model is always better." It is at least as good on the training points (with no penalty), which is not what matters.
  • "Overfitting shows up as a high training error." The training error is low. The sign is the gap between it and the test error.
  • "More regularisation is always safer." Too large a λ flattens the curve and underfits.

  2. Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning, Section 5.2, "Capacity, Overfitting and Underfitting", which compares linear, quadratic and degree-9 polynomial fits. Its Section 5.2.2, "Regularization", adds a weight decay penalty to the training MSE, in the same form as the ridge objective here, apart from leaving out the intercept.

About the data. The points are made up with the same recipe as Lesson 9. For each point in turn, x is drawn uniformly between −1 and 1, then y = sin(πx) plus 0.25 times a draw from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1, using mulberry32. The 15 training points use seed 3, so they are the first 15 of Lesson 9's 40 points; the 200 test points use seed 4. Step 5's extra training sets of 15 points use seeds 101 to 300; the spread quoted for degree 7 also uses five more blocks of 200 seeds, starting at 301, 501, 701, 901 and 1101. The points miss the wave only because of that noise; real data also has measurement error and things the model leaves out, and usually no known true curve. Fits use least squares, with the ridge penalty in step 6. Displayed values are rounded; the calculations are not.
