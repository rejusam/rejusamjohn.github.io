Overfitting and regularisation

A model can be too simple or too flexible. Both do badly on new data, for different reasons.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

We fit curves of rising flexibility to the same 15 points and score each one on 200 test points, as in Lesson 9. The training error keeps falling. The test error does not.

Then we look at why, through bias and variance, and at one fix: a penalty on large coefficients, called ridge regression.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • what underfitting and overfitting look like, in the curve and in the scores
  • what bias and variance mean, and how they add up to the test error
  • how a ridge penalty, with strength λ, calms a model that is too flexible

  1. Too simple: a straight line

    Start with a model too simple for the data: a straight line. Being too simple is called underfitting.

    Every curve here is a polynomial. Its degree is the highest power of x it uses. A straight line has degree 1, and higher degrees can bend more.

    The maths

    The data are y=sin⁡(πx)+εy = \sin(\pi x) + \varepsilony=sin(πx)+ε, with noise ε\varepsilonε of mean 0 and SD 0.25. A polynomial model of degree ppp is

    y^=θ0+θ1x+θ2x2+⋯+θpxp\hat{y} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \theta_2 x^2 + \dots + \theta_p x^py^​=θ0​+θ1​x+θ2​x2+⋯+θp​xp

    The curve bends, but the prediction is a weighted sum of the features x,x2,…,xpx, x^2, \dots, x^px,x2,…,xp, so it is linear in θ\boldsymbol{\theta}θ. The training MSE is the same kind of bowl as in Lesson 8, step 6, and least squares finds its lowest point exactly.

  2. About right: one hump, one dip

    Now the degree rises to 3. This curve can make one hump and one dip, the shape of the data, without chasing each point.

    For a new point, the error has two parts: the noise ε\varepsilonε, which no model can predict, and the gap between the fitted curve and sin⁡(πx)\sin(\pi x)sin(πx). The noise alone contributes 0.252=0.06250.25^2 = 0.06250.252=0.0625 to the expected test MSE. Step 5 splits the gap into two parts as well.

    The curve is not exactly the true one either: sin(πx) is not a polynomial of degree 3, so a small gap remains on top of the noise.

  3. Too flexible: chasing noise

    Now the degree jumps to 12. Its curve has 13 numbers to set, its coefficients (θs), for only 15 points.

    Look at the box: training MSE , the lowest of any degree here, but test MSE .

    The curve bends towards many training points, noise and all, and swings far from the pattern where there are none. This is overfitting. It does not show as a high training score. Its sign is the gap: low on training points, high on test points.

    Most of its test error comes from the right end, where by chance no training point fell. But even inside the training range, it does far worse on test points than degree 3.

  5. Bias and variance

    Why do both extremes fail? Refit each degree on many fresh training sets.

    The picture shows one degree fitted to 200 fresh sets of 15 points. The thin lines are 20 of the fits; the thick line is their average.

    Bias is how far the average fit sits from the true curve (dotted). With “Degree 1”, where it starts, the fits agree but all miss the bend. Variance is how much the fits disagree. With “Degree 7”, each fit follows its own noise and they spread widely. “Degree 3” keeps both small.

    The box measures both at x₀ = 0.5 (dashed line). There, the expected test error is bias² + variance + σ², the noise no model can remove. “Too small” means below what 200 fits can measure.

  6. Ridge: a penalty that calms

    Back to degree 12, now with a penalty set by the “λ” (lambda) slider.

    Ridge makes the training MSE plus λ times the sum of squared θs as small as possible. Wild swings need large θs, and the penalty holds them down. This is regularisation (in neural networks, weight decay).

Lesson complete

  • Too simple a model misses the pattern (underfitting). Too flexible a model learns the noise (overfitting). Both do badly on new data.
  • With no penalty, the training error never rises with the degree. The test error typically falls and then rises.
  • The expected test error at a point is bias squared plus variance plus the noise variance σ².
  • Ridge adds λ times the sum of the squared coefficients, leaving the intercept out. A well-chosen λ tames a flexible model.

Common misconceptions

  • "A more flexible model is always better." It is at least as good on the training points (with no penalty), which is not what matters.
  • "Overfitting shows up as a high training error." The training error is low. The sign is the gap between it and the test error.
  • "More regularisation is always safer." Too large a λ flattens the curve and underfits.

Further reading

  1. James, Witten, Hastie & Tibshirani, An Introduction to Statistical Learning (2nd ed.), Section 2.2.2, "The Bias-Variance Trade-Off", and Section 6.2.1, "Ridge Regression". Its ridge penalty is added to the sum of squared errors, so its λ is n times the one here.
  2. Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning, Section 5.2, "Capacity, Overfitting and Underfitting", which compares linear, quadratic and degree-9 polynomial fits. Its Section 5.2.2, "Regularization", adds a weight decay penalty to the training MSE, in the same form as the ridge objective here, apart from leaving out the intercept.

About the data. The points are made up with the same recipe as Lesson 9. For each point in turn, x is drawn uniformly between −1 and 1, then y = sin(πx) plus 0.25 times a draw from a normal distribution with mean 0 and SD 1, using mulberry32. The 15 training points use seed 3, so they are the first 15 of Lesson 9's 40 points; the 200 test points use seed 4. Step 5's extra training sets of 15 points use seeds 101 to 300; the spread quoted for degree 7 also uses five more blocks of 200 seeds, starting at 301, 501, 701, 901 and 1101. The points miss the wave only because of that noise; real data also has measurement error and things the model leaves out, and usually no known true curve. Fits use least squares, with the ridge penalty in step 6. Displayed values are rounded; the calculations are not.
