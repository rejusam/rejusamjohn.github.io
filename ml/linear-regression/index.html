Linear regression

Least squares chooses the straight line with the smallest total squared error.

About 12 minutes · No maths needed; the maths is there if you want it

Overview

Linear regression fits a straight line to points, so that the line can predict y from x. Lesson 1 showed a line like this being trained. This lesson opens it up.

You will place a line by eye first, and see its misses as squares. Then the least-squares formula finds the line with the smallest total area of squares. Lesson 1 promised to derive that formula, and Lesson 4 said it exists. Here it is.

The points are made up. See "About the data" at the end.

You'll learn

  • what least squares makes as small as possible, and how the best line is found
  • what the slope and the intercept of the fitted line mean, and where they stop meaning anything
  • what R² measures, and what it cannot tell you

  1. Which line?

  3. The squares are what we shrink

    Each squared miss is now drawn as a square on its stick. Least squares means making the total area of all the squares as small as possible.

    Each square's side is a miss, so its area is that miss squared. The SSE is the total area.

    Keep dragging the handles. A line that gets close to most points but far from a few still has big squares at those few. Squaring makes big misses count much more than small ones.

    The squares are drawn using the y-axis scale on both sides, so they look square on screen. That is a drawing choice; only their areas matter.

    Finding the smallest total by dragging is slow, and you can never be sure you have it. The next step finds it exactly.

    The slope says how much the prediction changes, on average, for each extra unit of x. The intercept is the prediction at x = 0, which may mean nothing if x = 0 lies outside the data.

  6. R²: how much of the spread the line explains

    R² compares the line's squared misses with the squared misses of the flat line at the mean of y. It is the share of the variation in y that the line accounts for.

    Press "Misses from the mean". The dashed sticks run to the flat line at the mean of y, the best constant guess from Lesson 4. Their squares add up to SST, the total sum of squares. It measures how much y varies before we use x at all.

    Press "Misses from the line". These sticks are the residuals again, and their squares add up to the SSE.

    R² = 1 − SSE/SST. If the line removes most of the misses, SSE is small next to SST and R² is near 1.

    Here SST = and SSE = , so R² = . The line accounts for about % of the variation in y.

    For the least-squares line with an intercept, R² is between 0 and 1 on the data it was fitted to (as long as y varies). The flat line at the mean is one of the lines least squares could choose, so its SSE can never be bigger than SST.

    R² has limits:

    • It does not show cause and effect.
    • It does not show that a straight line is the right shape. Points on a gentle curve can still give a high R².
    • It is computed on the same data the line was fitted to, so it says how well the line fits these points, not how well it will predict new ones. Lesson 9 deals with that.

Quick check

What does least squares make as small as possible?

Residuals above and below the line cancel. Every line through the point where x and y are both at their means makes them add up to zero, however badly it fits, so this cannot pick one line.

Least squares measures each miss straight up or down, in the direction of y, because the line is used to predict y from x. Perpendicular distances lead to a different method, which usually gives a different line.

Right. The SSE is the sum of the squared residuals, the total area of the squares, and least squares finds the line with the smallest one.

With scattered data, no straight line passes through most of the points, and counting ignores how far off each point is. Least squares uses the size of every miss.

What does the slope of a least-squares line tell you?

That is the intercept. It may mean nothing when x = 0 lies outside the data.

Right. Each step of one unit along x moves the prediction by the slope. It describes the trend in the data, and outside their range nothing backs it up.

That would be a claim about cause and effect. A fitted slope only describes how y and x go together in these data.

That is R². A steep slope can come with a low R², and a gentle slope with a high one.

Lesson complete

  • Least squares chooses the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals, the SSE. No other straight line does better on these data.
  • For one input it has a closed form, b^=Sxy/Sxx\hat{b} = S_{xy}/S_{xx}b^=Sxy​/Sxx​ and a^=yˉ−b^ xˉ\hat{a} = \bar{y} - \hat{b}\,\bar{x}a^=yˉ​−b^xˉ, found by setting both partial derivatives of the SSE to zero.
  • The slope is the average change in the prediction per unit of x within the data. R² = 1 − SSE/SST is the share of the variation in y the line accounts for.

Common misconceptions

  • "Least squares minimises the distance from each point to the line." It minimises squared vertical distances, the misses in y.
  • "A high R² proves the line is right." It does not show cause and effect, and a curved pattern can still give a high R².
  • "The intercept always means something." It is the prediction at x = 0, which may be outside the data or impossible.
  • "Least squares only works if the data are normal." Computing the line needs only that x has spread. Assumptions such as normal errors matter for exact confidence intervals and tests in small samples.
